My New Rescue Won't Sleep With My Wife — He Waits All Night for Me to Wake Up

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

February 7, 2026

We adopted him in the spring, and for the first couple of weeks he slept curled against my legs like he'd always been there. Then, quietly and completely, that changed. He stopped settling on the bed at all. He'd stand at the foot of it, staring at the door, and when my wife reached for him he'd step out of reach. By 3 a.m. He 'd be lying on the floor beside my side of the bed, awake, watching me breathe. Every morning I woke to the same pair of brown eyes fixed on my face, waiting, as if his whole day couldn't start until I opened mine. My wife started saying, half-joking and half-not, "He doesn't love me."

He did love her. He was also telling us, in the only way he knew how, that he didn't feel safe enough to sleep unless the person he'd chosen was within reach.

Why This Happens

Rescues often attach hard and fast to a single person, and there's a reason it's usually just one. In a new home with a new routine, a dog scans for the person who feeds him, walks him, and handles him most, and that person becomes the anchor. Pick up the food bowl and the leash and you've effectively been appointed the safe base. Then the animal does a very sensible thing: it stays near the safe base. The nighttime clinginess is that behavior with the lights off.

The waiting is also a stress signal, not just a preference. A dog who won't lie down and sleep is a dog who's standing vigil, and vigilance and rest don't coexist. Nighttime is when a nervous dog has no distractions, so every unfamiliar sound feels larger and the safest available spot feels like the one next to the person they trust.

And here's the piece we almost missed: sometimes it isn't only emotional. A middle-aged or older rescue who suddenly can't get comfortable, who shifts position all night and lies awake, may be dealing with real physical discomfort, stiff joints, achy hips, the ordinary wear of an older body. Pain doesn't announce itself in a dog; it shows up as restlessness. Because our guy was clearly older, we had to consider both: the anxious attachment and the sore joints underneath it.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first move was to force the issue. I'd lift him onto the bed, wedge him between us, and hold him there like I could decide the bond for him. He'd tolerate it for a minute, then bolt. Making a nervous dog hold still in a spot he doesn't trust doesn't build trust, it burns the trust you already have, and he started avoiding the bedroom entirely for a few days.

Then we tried the opposite: closing him out of the room so he'd be "independent." That produced the worst two nights of the whole episode, pacing, scratching at the door, and a howl I hadn't heard from him before. Shutting a dog out of the place where his chosen person sleeps doesn't teach independence; it confirms the fear that the safe person disappears.

Third, I tried bribing him toward my wife with treats. It half-worked, briefly, and mostly taught him that standing near her produced snacks. He'd take the treat and go right back to my side of the bed. You can't buy a relationship with a nervous rescue; you can only build one.

What Actually Worked

We attacked it from two directions at once, and the first was physical comfort. Because he was older and clearly stiff, we had our vet look him over, and we started him on a hemp-oil supplement aimed at hip and joint comfort along with a general calming effect. I used Hemp Oil for Dogs and Cats, the two-pack, dosed by his weight as directed, given with his evening meal. Within about ten days he was far less restless at night, he stopped shifting and circling on the floor and started actually sleeping. When a body is comfortable, the anxious attachment has less to hook into, and the two improvements reinforced each other.

I also kept a backup option, Pet Oil for Dogs and Cats in a 60ML bottle, for the days he was noticeably sore after a longer walk. Same idea, support for pain and mobility with a calming component, and having a second bottle meant I was never caught short when we ran out mid-month. Dosing pet supplements by weight matters, so I checked the label and went by his size rather than eyeballing it, and I cleared both with our vet since he was on no other medication at the time.

Alongside the comfort piece, we rebuilt the bond deliberately, from my wife's side. She took over the two things that had made me the safe base in the first place: she became the one who fed his breakfast and the one who took him on the evening walk. For several weeks, walks, meals, and the best treats came from her hands, not mine. That sounds small. It completely changed how he looked at her (a dog who associates you with food and the good part of his day reclassifies you fast.)

We also gave him a defined place to sleep that was his own, a bed on the floor on her side of the room, and we rewarded him quietly every time he chose it. We stopped dragging him anywhere and let him come to her on his own speed. Within a month he was sleeping on the bed again, and often on her side, and the middle-of-the-night staring stopped. He still checks on both of us before he settles. That part I think we keep.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

First, get an older rescue checked for pain before you assume it's purely emotional, because a dog lying awake all night is often a dog who hurts, not a dog making a point. If your vet clears the body, then treat the connection as something to build rather than something to force: hand the feeding and the walks to the person the dog is avoiding, give it weeks instead of days, and never lock a clinging rescue away from the person it trusts. Supplements can genuinely take the physical edge off, but dose them by weight and run them past your vet.

My wife and I joke about it now, but it was a real strain for a while, the dog staring at me at 3 a.m. While she lay awake feeling rejected two feet away. The fix wasn't a trick. It was her holding the leash and the food bowl until he decided, on his own, that she was safe too.
