My 14-Week-Old Puppy Wakes Me Twice a Night to Go Out — I Nearly Gave Up

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

November 26, 2025

By the third week of waking up at 1:40 a.m. And again at 4:15 a.m. To a puppy standing in his crate crying, I was a zombie, and I was genuinely starting to wonder if I had made a terrible mistake. Copper is a 14-week-old puppy with the sweetest face in the world and a bladder the size of a walnut, and I was so sleep-deprived that I forgot my own phone number at the pharmacy.

Night potty training is the part people gloss over when they tell you to get a puppy. Everyone warns you about the chewing and the vet bills. Nobody sits you down and explains that you will genuinely lose sleep for a while. Here is what I learned about the difference between a puppy who needs to go out at night and one who has learned to wake you for company (and how I finally got us both sleeping.)

Why This Happens

At 14 weeks, Copper physically cannot hold it all night. Puppies this age can manage roughly an hour for every month of age, so three to three and a half hours is realistic, and a full eight or nine hours is not. Two to three night wake-ups for a puppy this young is normal, not a sign that something is broken or that he will never be housetrained. His bladder simply has not grown into the job yet.

There is a second layer, though, and this is the one that sneaks up on you. As puppies get older, a night wake-up can shift from a physical need into a habit. If every time he cries you come in, turn on the lights, talk to him, and give him a little attention, he learns that crying at 2 a.m. Is a reliable way to summon you. So part of the problem is a small bladder, and part of it is a puppy who has figured out the system.

There is also a genuine medical possibility worth ruling out. If a puppy is suddenly going far more often than his age predicts, or if you notice accidents despite frequent outings, a urinary tract infection is something a vet will usually want to check before you assume it is behavioral. It is common and easy to miss, so it is worth a phone call if the frequency seems off.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first attempt was to cut off his water after dinner, thinking less in meant less out. That is a mistake I would not repeat. Puppies need water, and restricting it can cause its own problems, plus Copper just drank frantically during the day to make up for it. It is fine to pull the water bowl an hour or two before bed, but cutting it off all evening is not the fix.

Then I tried "cry it out." I read that if I just ignored him, he would settle. For a hungry, full-bladder puppy this young, that did not build independence, it built distress, and it kept the whole household awake. I was not teaching him to sleep; I was teaching him that nobody came.

My third failed experiment was picking him up and cuddling him back to sleep in my bed whenever he cried. That fixed the crying for exactly as long as it took him to realize the bed was now the goal, after which the 2 a.m. Cries became a nightly request for a transfer, and I had accidentally taught him to wake me on purpose.

What Actually Worked

The system that finally worked started with a late potty run right before I went to bed, ideally around 10 or 11 p.m., so he went into the crate genuinely empty. I pulled his water bowl about two hours before that, which is enough time for him to hydrate without a full bladder at midnight. That single change eliminated the first wake-up almost immediately.

I also fixed the crate itself. It needs to be just big enough for him to stand, turn, and lie down, no bigger. A crate with too much room gives a puppy a corner to potty in, and a puppy who can potty in his own space is a puppy who will. Once Copper's crate fit him properly, he stopped wanting to soil it.

For the remaining night wake-up, the trick was to be boring and to preempt. I set an alarm for just before his usual time, took him out on a leash with almost no light and no talking, let him potty, praised quietly, and put him straight back. No play, no snuggle, no lights that felt like morning. Then I pushed that alarm later and later over a couple of weeks until it faded into a single early-morning run. Puppies get the memo when the night trip is the most boring part of the day.

I also gave him a real wind-down routine so he went into the crate already calm instead of wired. A lick mat smeared with a little something and a heartbeat-comfort puppy that gives him the sense that he is not alone in the dark made a huge difference for a puppy who was still missing his littermates at night. Those two together turned "alone and scared" into "settled and sleepy," and the crating went from a fight to a routine.

One more piece, for a related problem: Copper had started barking at every little noise in the house at night, which fed the wake-ups. A humane ultrasonic deterrent, used only when he fixated on a sound, interrupted that pattern long enough for me to redirect him and settle him. It is a small tool, not a solution on its own, but it stopped the barking from snowballing into a full night awake.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Know that two or three night wake-ups at 14 weeks is normal, and that it does end, usually by four or five months if you are consistent. Do the late-night potty, size the crate correctly, keep the night trip dull as dishwater, and stop giving attention in the middle of the night. If he is going far more often than his age predicts, call your vet about his urine before you blame yourself.

Copper sleeps through the night now, mostly, and the difference in my own life is ridiculous. I was not a terrible owner for being exhausted. I was just a person trying to potty-train a baby on three hours of sleep, and once the system replaced the willpower, we both finally got to sleep.
