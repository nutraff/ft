My Rabbit's Ear Infection Kept Coming Back — Here's What Finally Got It Under Control

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

August 30, 2025

I noticed it during a head scritch. Biscuit, my Holland Lop, usually melts when I rub behind his ears, but that morning he flinched away and then shook his head hard, like something was stuck in there. When I looked closer, the inside of his ear was crusty and dark, and there was a faint, sour smell I hadn't noticed before. Over the next two days he was tilting his head slightly to one side and scratching at that ear with his back foot until the fur around it was thin.

My first thought was that a lop rabbit's ears just collect dirt, and I could clean it myself. That's what led to the second infection. I spent a week scrubbing at his ear with cotton swabs, and it looked a little better, so I stopped. Ten days later it was worse than ever, more discharge, more head shaking, and now he was reluctant to eat. That's when I learned the hard way that rabbit ears are not a do-it-yourself job.

Why This Happens

Rabbits, especially lops, are built with an unfortunate anatomy problem. Their ear canals fold downward and inward, which means moisture and debris don't drain the way they do in an upright-eared animal. That trapped warmth and dampness is basically a welcome mat for bacteria, and many rabbit ear infections trace back to bacteria like Pasteurella, which is common in rabbits and opportunistic. Once it takes hold in a warm, dark, poorly ventilated canal, it can go deep.

The lops have it worse than most, and I didn't fully appreciate that when I picked Biscuit. A folded ear is cute, but it's a canal that barely breathes. Add bedding dust, a little wax, and humidity, and you've got the conditions an infection needs. This is why some lops seem to battle ear trouble their whole lives while upright-eared rabbits never have a single issue.

The mistake I made was treating the visible crust and calling it done. The infection wasn't on the outer fold, it was deeper, where I could not safely reach with a swab. Every time I poked at the surface with a cotton tip, I was nudging debris farther down and possibly scraping the delicate lining, which just gave the bacteria another opening. Vets warn against sticking anything into a rabbit's ear canal, and now I understand why.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first attempt was cotton swabs, and it was a mistake from the start. Q-tips can push wax and debris deeper, and a sudden flinch can drive one into a canal that is far more fragile than it looks. I'm lucky I didn't do real damage.

Next I tried human ear drops, reasoning that if it worked for swimmers' ear it would work for a rabbit. That was worse than useless, the extra liquid sat in that folded canal and made the damp environment even more infection-friendly. It also stung, and Biscuit spent the next hour trying to get away from his own ear.

I also tried just leaving it alone to see if his body would sort it out. Rabbits don't reliably clear an established ear infection on their own, and while I waited, the discharge got thicker and his appetite dipped. In a rabbit, appetite dipping is a red flag, because it's the first step toward gut stasis. That was the point where I stopped experimenting and called the vet.

What Actually Worked

The fix started with a proper diagnosis and a course of prescription medicated drops from my vet, which is the only thing that actually treats an infection living deep in the canal. No over-the-counter product was ever going to fix that, and I want to be clear about that up front. What I could do at home was keep the outside clean without pushing anything inward, and that's where the right tools finally made a difference.

Instead of fingertips or swabs, I used the 2 Pcs 10.6" Stainless Steel Reptile Feeding Tongs & Tweezers to hold a soft cotton pad and wipe only the outer ear folds and the entrance to the canal. The long stainless tongs gave me reach and control, my fingers stayed away from the delicate opening, and I could see exactly where the pad was going. It sounds like an odd repurposing, but the precision of a long slim tool is exactly what you want for cleaning the shallow folds of a folded ear without going where you shouldn't. Pad in, wipe the outside, done.

The other issue was the mess the discharge made. It was staining his bedding and leaving a smell in the corner where he slept, and no amount of regular laundry was getting it out. I used the Rocco & Roxie Extreme Enzyme Stain & Odor Eliminator Spray on his bedding and the carpet around his pen, and the enzyme formula actually broke down the organic residue instead of just covering it with perfume. Once the crusted discharge was gone from his space, keeping his ears clean stopped feeling like a losing battle, because he wasn't lying in a re-infected nest every night.

The final piece was prevention: I switched his bedding to something less dusty and kept his pen drier, and I now check the inside of his ears once a week so I catch a flare before it becomes a week-long problem. Clearing the infection for real, keeping the outer ear clean safely, keeping his environment dry, and checking weekly (that combination is what finally ended the cycle.)

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

If your rabbit is head-shaking, tilting, or has crusty, smelly ears, go to the vet first and clean nothing at home until you have a diagnosis. An established infection needs prescription treatment, and every day you spend experimenting with home remedies is a day the bacteria gets deeper into a canal that's hard to reach. I lost ten days learning that.

Then clean only the outside, carefully, with something that keeps your fingers out of the ear, and never, ever push a swab into the canal. Keep the bedding dry and low-dust, since dampness is the fuel that feeds every recurrence. Watch the appetite as closely as the ear, because a rabbit that stops eating is a bigger emergency than any infection. And check weekly, so the next flare is a two-day problem instead of a two-week one.
