We Moved Houses and My Ten-Month Poodle Started Peeing Indoors Again — Here's What Settled Her

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

January 12, 2026

My poodle was fully house-trained by six months. She was the dog I bragged about. Then we moved into a new house in the fall, and by the second day she was peeing on the corner of the bedroom carpet like she'd never been trained a day in her life. Not in the middle of the floor where I might miss it, always in a corner, always on something soft, always right after I'd turned my back. Ten months old and reliably clean for four months, undone by a change of address.

The first few accidents felt like a fluke. By the end of the first week I was mopping up two a day and wondering whether the move had somehow broken her, or whether I'd been fooling myself about her progress all along.

Why This Happens

A move is one of the biggest stressors a dog can experience, and it hits hardest on the things they rely on for security: smell, routine, and territory. Her whole world, every familiar scent, the layout she'd memorized, the exact spot by the door where she always went, vanished in an afternoon. In the new house, nothing smelled like home, and for a dog, scent is home.

There's also an age factor. Ten months is adolescence, a stage where training that felt rock solid suddenly goes soft. Add a move on top of a developmental regression and you've got a dog whose training is being pulled apart from two directions at once. It's not defiance. It's overwhelm.

And then there's the marking piece, which is closely tied to stress. In an unfamiliar house full of unfamiliar smells, a dog may urinate to put her own scent down and claim the space, especially in corners and on soft surfaces, which hold scent better. Every accident I didn't fully clean made the next one more likely, because to her nose the corner already smelled like a bathroom.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first instinct was to confine her to the crate whenever I couldn't watch her, thinking I could just wait out the phase. But crating an already stressed dog in an unfamiliar room made the anxiety worse, not better, and she'd sometimes pee the second I let her out. The exact opposite of what the crate is supposed to do.

Then I did the thing I'm not proud of: I scolded her. I came around the corner, saw her mid-squat, and snapped at her. She stopped, slunk away, and, naturally, started going where I couldn't see. I'd traded a visible problem for a hidden one, which is a much worse problem.

My third attempt was denial. I cleaned the spots with a regular all-purpose spray that smelled clean to me, told myself she'd settle in a week, and changed nothing else. The spots kept calling her back. A citrus deodorizer doesn't remove the proteins a dog's nose can still detect, so from her perspective I was basically leaving a welcome mat down.

What Actually Worked

The first real step was recreating her routine from scratch. I put her back on a schedule as tight as the one we'd used when she was a young puppy, out first thing, after every meal, after every nap, before I left, and right before bed, so her body had a predictable rhythm in the new space. I picked one potty spot in the new yard and walked her to it on a leash every time so it would start to smell like hers. Within a week that patch was clearly "the spot," and she'd head straight for it.

I also cleaned like it actually mattered. Enzymatic cleaner on every accident, soaked in, blotted, and left to dry, including the carpet pad where the smell had sunk in. That one change removed the invisible invitation the corners had been sending her. Once the old spots stopped smelling like a bathroom, her reasons for revisiting them fell apart.

Then I addressed the anxiety itself, because the accidents were a symptom of a nervous dog, and a nervous dog doesn't learn well. My vet and I talked through calming support, and I added VUN Calming Chews for Dog, a daily chew aimed at stress relief and settling. I gave them in the morning, when her pacing was worst, and over about a week she stopped patrolling the house looking for something to worry about and started actually resting. A dog who can rest is a dog whose body can hold it and whose brain can learn the new rules.

I kept a second option in rotation too. VetriScience Calm & Confident Chews for Dogs in bacon, which I reached for on the especially restless days and when we had visitors, since another round of strangers in the new house sent her right back up. Between the two, I had something for the ordinary daily edge and something for the bigger wobbles, and having both meant I wasn't scrambling every time she had a rough day. She takes the bacon ones eagerly, which makes the whole thing easy.

Finally, I gave her one small room to start as her safe base instead of the run of the whole house, and expanded her access as she got confident. Denning down in a familiar-smelling space, with a bed and a couple of her old toys, gave her a reset button in a house that otherwise felt foreign.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Expect a regression, not a disaster. A move resets a dog's sense of home, and the fix is to rebuild routine, scent, and security from the ground up rather than waiting for her to "get used to it." Rule out a urinary infection with your vet if the behavior is new, frequent, or paired with straining, that's a same-day call, not a training issue. Then clean every old spot with an enzyme cleaner, put her back on a puppy-tight schedule, keep her world small at first, and give her a real hand with the anxiety.

Calming chews aren't a sedative and they aren't a cure, they just take enough of the edge off that training can actually land. Give it a few weeks. By the end of the first month in our new house, my poodle was clean again and back to being the dog I bragged about. She just needed the new place to feel like hers.,
