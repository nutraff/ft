My Maltese's Breath Could Knock You Over — Here's the At-Home Routine That Fixed It

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

July 19, 2025

My Maltese, Coco, is a lap dog. That is the whole point of a Maltese. She sleeps against my chest, sits on my knees through movies, and greets me by jumping up and pushing her nose right into my face. Which is exactly how I found out, one excruciating morning about two years ago, that her breath had gone from "dog breath" to something genuinely foul. Not just strong. Sour, metallic, like something was rotting in there. I flinched. She noticed. And I started Googling "Maltese bad breath" with my heart in my throat.

Here is the thing that made it worse before it got better: small toy breeds like Maltese are some of the most prone to dental disease of any dogs. Their teeth are packed tightly into a tiny mouth, which leaves all sorts of pockets for food and bacteria to collect, and they live long enough that the problem has decades to develop. I had been feeding Coco soft food, brushing "occasionally," and hoping. That was the entire plan, and it was not a plan.

Why This Happens

Dog bad breath is almost never just "dog breath." In the overwhelming majority of cases, it comes from the mouth itself, and usually from the gums. When plaque, the soft film of bacteria that forms on teeth within hours of eating, is not disturbed, it mineralizes into tartar in a matter of days. Tartar is rough, so it gives the bacteria even more surface to cling to, and that bacterial load is what produces the smell. A mouth with advanced tartar smells like decay because, in a real sense, it is decaying.

Under the gumline is where it gets dangerous, and it is the part you cannot see. The bacteria there trigger inflammation that gradually destroys the tissue and bone holding the teeth in. This is periodontal disease, and it is the most common condition vets diagnose in adult dogs. Because it hides below the gum, owners usually notice nothing until the smell is severe. Bad breath is often the first real symptom, which means by the time you smell it, the process has been going on for a long while.

For a breed like the Maltese, two things stack the odds against you. One is the crowded small mouth, which creates conditions for plaque to take hold. The other is the soft-food, lapdog lifestyle, no gnawing on anything tough, no chewing that would scrub the teeth, and a diet that coats them in residue. Coco was a textbook case. Every factor that leads to dental disease, she had.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

The first thing I tried was breath fresheners. Little chewable mint tablets, water additives, a spray for her mouth. They masked the smell for about an hour and did nothing about the actual cause. I was spraying perfume on a problem that needed cleaning, and I let it distract me from the real issue for weeks.

Then I tried dental treats and chews. This is closer to right in principle, because chewing does provide mechanical cleaning, but most of the chews Coco would accept were soft and gummy. She would swallow them in seconds without really chewing, so they scrubbed almost nothing. And the hard ones, the ones that might have worked, I was nervous about, because a hard chew can crack a weakened tooth, especially on a small dog. I was stuck between useless and risky.

Finally I tried a regular human toothbrush, which was a disaster. The long handle does not fit a small dog's mouth, I could not angle it to reach the gumline where the problem actually lives, and Coco, who weighs seven pounds, treated it as a foreign object and fought me every second. I gave up after three nights. The tool was wrong and my approach was wrong, and giving up left her mouth exactly where it started.

What Actually Worked

The piece that turned it around was switching to a finger toothbrush. Once I slid my finger, wearing a soft silicone cap, along Coco's gumline, I could suddenly feel what I was missing, the roughed-up tartar near the gum, the spots that needed the most attention, and reach them with tiny motions instead of wrestling a stick. The Jasper 360° Finger Toothbrush is the one I use: the bristles circle the whole tip, so a back-and-forth rub cleans the outside of the teeth without me having to rotate anything, and the silicone is gentle on a small dog's delicate gums. I introduced it slowly, letting her lick puppy toothpaste off my finger for a few days first, then brushing for ten seconds and stopping while she was still relaxed. Within two weeks she was sitting still for a full pass on both sides. I keep a couple on hand, they are small, they get dropped, and I never want to skip a night because I misplaced it.

The second change was giving her something to chew that actually did something. Brushing cleans the outside of the teeth, but the back teeth and the gaps between them need work a finger cannot reach, and that is where a good chew comes in. I started rotating in firmer chew toys so she had a reason to gnaw every day, and I weaned her off the soft food toward a mix that gave her teeth some resistance. A daily chew plus a nightly brush covered both the surfaces and the in-between.

Within about six weeks the sour smell was gone. Not masked, gone, because the stuff causing it was getting disrupted every single day. Her last vet visit, the technician commented on how clean her teeth looked, and no cleaning was recommended. That was the payoff, and it cost me about ninety seconds a night.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Treat bad breath as a dental symptom, not a cosmetic one. Do not reach for breath fresheners, reach for a brush and get your finger on the gumline, where the problem lives. Start slow with dog toothpaste, keep the sessions under a minute, and reward. Add a daily chew that your dog actually chews, not one it swallows whole, and be careful with anything hard enough to crack a tooth. For a small breed, this is not optional maintenance; it is the difference between a healthy mouth and years of slow, hidden pain.

If your dog's breath has a strong sweetness or a rotten smell, or if you see red, bleeding, or swollen gums, see a vet, some of that needs a professional cleaning, and a little home care is what keeps it from coming back. Coco is back to pushing her nose in my face every morning. I just do not flinch anymore.
