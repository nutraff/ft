My 90-Pound Dog Was Walking Me — Not the Other Way Around, Until I Fixed Two Pieces of Gear

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

August 10, 2026

The squirrel made the decision for both of us. My ninety-pound lab mix spotted it across the street, and in about half a second I went from a person walking a dog to a water-skier being towed through my own neighborhood. My shoulder popped, my wrist burned, and I spent the next block apologizing to a stranger whose little terrier we had nearly mowed down on the sidewalk.

I'd owned big dogs before, or so I told myself, and I honestly believed the pulling was just a training problem I hadn't cracked yet. So I spent months drilling commands while my arm got longer and my shoulder started aching every morning. Here's the thing I wish someone had told me sooner: with a strong dog, the gear and the training are one system, and fixing only one of them never holds.

Why This Happens

A dog pulls because pulling works. Every step he drags you forward is a reward, the walk continues, the smell gets closer, the squirrel stays in sight. He isn't being stubborn or trying to dominate you. He's doing exactly what a smart animal does, which is repeat the behavior that pays off. The leash tension you feel isn't the problem; it's the evidence of a habit that has been reinforced a few thousand times.

There's also a physical reflex working against you. Fit a dog in a flat collar and lean into the leash, and most dogs push forward into that pressure instead of yielding to it. Trainers call it the opposition reflex, and it's why a tight leash often seems to make a dog pull harder. A collar around the throat also concentrates your dog's entire strength on one small, fragile area, which is bad for him and useless for you as a steering wheel.

Then there's the use problem, and this is the piece that finally clicked for me. A standard back-clip harness is basically a sled-dog rig. It puts your dog's strongest muscles, his chest and shoulders, directly against the leash and gives him a comfortable platform to lean into. Perfect for a husky hauling a sled. Terrible if the human on the other end weighs less than the dog.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first attempt was pure technique: the classic "stop every time the leash goes tight" move. In theory, stopping removes the reward for pulling. In practice, on a dog this size, I couldn't stop him. He simply kept walking and towed me another ten feet while I dug in my heels. The method isn't wrong, but it needs a dog you can actually hold, and I couldn't hold mine.

Then a friend handed me a prong collar "just to try." I put it on, watched my dog's whole posture collapse into something tense and shut-down, and took it off within the hour. I won't claim it never works for anyone, but it wasn't the relationship I wanted with my dog, and it never taught him what I wanted him to do instead.

My third mistake was a retractable leash, which felt like freedom and behaved like a winch. A retractable leash keeps constant tension on the dog no matter what, which is the exact opposite of the loose leash I was trying to teach, and it gave him a running start every single time. I finally tried a plain back-clip harness thinking it would stop the choking. It stopped the choking and doubled the pulling, because I had just handed a sled dog his rig.

What Actually Worked

The turning point was switching to a front-clip, escape-proof harness. It has a loop on the chest, and when my dog surges, the leash gently turns his whole body toward me instead of letting him lean into a sled-style haul. The one I settled on also has a sturdy top handle, which is a quiet turning point, in traffic or around other dogs I can grab that handle and have real, immediate control, like the handle on a suitcase instead of a rope around my wrist.

The Huntboo Escape Proof Dog Harness is the one that ended up living by my door. The "escape proof" part matters more than it sounds: earlier harnesses had slipped off over his head mid-pull, which nearly cost me a dog in the street, and this design cinches around the body so he can't back out of it. It's padded, which ended the chafing the cheap nylon versions gave him, and the reflective stitching means drivers actually see us on our early walks.

Then I paired it with the other half of the system: a hands-free bungee leash that clips to a waist belt. The Tuff Pupper Hands Free Bungee Dog Leash changed the physics of the whole walk. Instead of my arm fighting his neck, my hips and center of gravity absorb the pull, and the built-in stretch smooths out the sudden jolts so a lunge no longer yanks my shoulder. Having both hands free also meant I could finally reward him. A treat delivered at the exact moment he walked politely taught him more in a week than months of leash-yanking ever had.

Gear alone still wasn't enough, so I bolted on two simple habits. First, I rewarded the position I wanted: when he walked with a loose leash beside me, the treats and praise came fast, so "staying near me" became the behavior that paid. Second, I stopped letting him drag me into every bush, if he surged, we turned and took a few steps the other way, which quietly taught him that pulling moved us farther from the smell, not closer. The harness gave me control, the bungee leash gave me a free hand, and the treats made him want to cooperate.

Fit matters as much as the model. A harness that's too loose slides and rubs; one that's too tight pinches at the shoulders. I measured him across the chest and around the ribs, went snug but with room for two fingers under the straps, and rechecked every couple of weeks while he was still filling out.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

If your dog outweighs your ability to hold him, fix the gear first, because no training plan survives a dog who is physically towing you. Get a front-clip harness that fits snugly and won't slip off, add a hands-free leash if you've ever felt your shoulder wrench, and then layer in the reward work once you can finally hold your ground.

My dog still loves squirrels. But these days when one darts across the road, he hits the end of the leash, feels the harness turn him, looks back at me, and earns a treat. He isn't perfect, and neither is my shoulder. Still, the walk belongs to both of us again, and that's worth every dollar I spent on the right two pieces of gear.
