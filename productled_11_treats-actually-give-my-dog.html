The Treats I Actually Give My Dog — and Why the Cheap Ones Won

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

December 9, 2024

I spent about seventy dollars on training treats in my first month with my dog, and the ones that ended up doing all the work cost less than a bag of chips. That's the part nobody puts in the Instagram post. You read "use high-value treats," you picture salmon and freeze-dried liver and some artisanal thing in a linen pouch, and you assume the cheap stuff is a compromise. It isn't. It's the workhorse.

Here's what actually happened: I bought a bag of soft, smelly training bites that cost more than my own lunch, used them for three days, and watched my dog's attention drift the second a squirrel existed. Then, out of desperation on a rainy Tuesday, I grabbed a cheap bag of crunchy puppy bites from the grocery store, and she locked in like I'd flipped a switch. Same dog. Same street. Different bag.

Why This Happens

Treat value isn't about price. It's about three things dogs actually care about: smell, texture, and swallow time. A treat that smells strongly gets noticed. A soft treat can be eaten in one bite, which means you can reward fast and get back to training. A hard or crumbly treat takes several seconds to chew, and during those seconds your dog has stopped paying attention to you, and you've stopped being able to reward the next good thing.

That last point is the one I got wrong for weeks. Training works on timing. If your dog does the right thing and you spend eight seconds digging a biscuit out of a pouch while she crumbles it on the sidewalk, the reward lands too late to attach to the behavior. You're not training anymore; you're just feeding her on a walk and hoping something sticks.

There's also a value hierarchy people miss. For a dog, a treat isn't "good" or "bad" in the abstract. It 's good or bad relative to what's competing for her attention. A crunchy biscuit is plenty for practicing "sit" in your kitchen. It is nowhere near enough for "leave the dead squirrel alone" on a live city street. The mistake is using one treat for everything, then blaming the dog when the low-stakes food loses to the high-stakes distraction.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first move was to buy the most expensive bag on the shelf, on the theory that better ingredients would equal better focus. The ingredients were genuinely better. The focus was not. My dog liked them fine, but they were hard and dry, so every reward took her four or five chews. I'd ask for a behavior, she'd nail it, and then we'd both stand there for five seconds while she finished the biscuit. By the time she was ready again, whatever I was trying to teach had evaporated.

Then I tried using her regular kibble as a training reward, because every guide says you can just take a scoop of her dinner on the walk and reward with that. This is technically true and practically useless if your dog is food-motivated but also aware that there's a bag of better stuff in your pocket. She knew the difference. She'd take the kibble, look at me, and wait for the real thing. Training became a standoff.

The third thing I tried was the opposite: only ever using the fancy treats, so everything was high value. That killed the novelty. A reward only feels special if it's not the everyday default. My dog got so used to the good stuff that it stopped being a reward and started being a snack. Her responsiveness slid right back to where it started.

What Actually Worked

The fix was a two-tier system, and it's the single most useful training habit I've built. Tier one is the everyday reward: small, soft, cheap, and eaten fast. I landed on soft training bites that break into pieces and go down in one bite. Tier two is the emergency reward: something genuinely exciting that only comes out for the hard stuff. Big distractions, scary situations, new environments.

The everyday tier doesn't need to be fancy, but it does need two qualities: it has to be soft enough to swallow quickly, and it has to be cut small. I got a bag of grain-free chicken training bites that are basically the size of a pencil eraser and cost less per treat than anything else in the cabinet. Because they're tiny, I can reward ten times in a minute without overfeeding her, which matters more than people realize, you're going to hand out a lot of rewards in a real session, and if each one is a full biscuit you've fed a meal by accident.

For the emergency tier, I keep a small bag of single-ingredient freeze-dried salmon. It's the one thing that reliably beats a squirrel. I ration it hard, because the whole point is that it's rare. When it comes out, my dog knows something real is happening.

The other part of what worked was mechanical, and it took me embarrassingly long to figure out: I stopped using a deep pouch and started using one I could reach into without looking. A magnetic-closure pouch worn at the waist means my hand is in and out in under a second. That change alone tightened my timing enough that my dog started learning faster, using the exact same treats. The reward wasn't the problem. My delivery speed was.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Buy two treats, not one. A cheap, soft, tiny everyday reward you can hand out a hundred times a week without thinking, and one genuinely special treat you guard like it's currency. Then practice your delivery speed in your kitchen before you ever take it outside. Because the treat that matters is the one that appears within a second of your dog doing the right thing, and it doesn't matter how good it tastes if it shows up late.

And ignore the price tag when you're choosing. Some of the cheapest treats I've bought have out-performed the expensive ones by a mile, because they were soft, smelly, and fast. The expensive bag is still in the cabinet. The cheap one is the one I keep re-buying.
