My Ten-Year-Old Rescue Pooped Inside Every Day for a Month — What Finally Broke the Cycle

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

January 8, 2026

The first week with my ten-year-old rescue, I told myself it was just the transition. New house, new people, strange smells, of course she'd need time. But by week three she was still circling the living room every afternoon and leaving a pile behind the armchair, sometimes within an hour of being outside. She'd look at me afterward with her head low, not defiant, just sad, and I'd clean it up and feel my patience fraying a little more each time. By week four I was standing over a bucket of hot water at midnight thinking, I don't know if I can do this.

What kept me going was reminding myself who she was. This wasn't a puppy who'd never learned. This was an old dog with a rough past who had been through more upheaval in a month than most dogs see in a lifetime, and she was doing her best in a body and mind that had both been through the wringer.

Why This Happens

When an older dog starts pooping inside, the honest first step is a vet visit, because senior dogs have senior problems that masquerade as behavior issues. Arthritis makes it painful to get up and get to the door in time. Digestive changes and new food can loosen things up. And cognitive dysfunction (the canine version of what humans call dementia, genuinely scrambles a dog's sense of routine and location. I had her checked first, and it's the reason I could stop blaming her and start actually solving it.)

Once medical causes were off the table, the explanation came down to stress and history. A rescue with a trauma background often arrives without a reliable house-training foundation, or with a foundation that was built on punishment and collapsed under pressure. Anxiety loosens bowels directly, the gut is sensitive to stress in dogs just as it is in people, and a dog who feels unsafe is a dog who can't reliably hold it, especially when left alone.

There's also a routine gap. She'd spent months in a shelter on a rigid schedule set by other people, and at ten years old, unlearning and relearning is slower than it is for a puppy. I was expecting a fast reset from a dog who could only do a slow one.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

The first thing I tried was punishment, and it was the worst thing I did. I scolded her, pointed at the mess, and made my frustration obvious. All that produced was a dog who started hiding behind the couch to poop where I couldn't see it. Punishment doesn't build a new habit; it just teaches a dog to conceal the old one. It took me weeks to earn back the trust I burned in that one afternoon.

Next I tried putting her in a crate whenever I couldn't watch her. That contained the mess but taught her nothing, and for a dog with her history a crate she hadn't been gradually introduced to felt like a cage. She paced and panted in it, and the accidents just moved to the moment I let her out.

Then I tried feeding her once early in the morning so everything would "clear out" before I left for work. It didn't work, because I didn't pair it with a matching potty schedule, I'd just moved the input without moving the output window. She was still alone at the wrong time of day, still anxious, still having accidents.

What Actually Worked

What finally turned it around was slowing down and treating her like the individual she is. I built a rigid schedule and stuck to it: out first thing, out after every meal, out before I left and the second I got home, and one last trip right before bed. Same door, same spots, quiet and unhurried. An anxious old dog needs predictability more than she needs novelty, and a schedule she could set a clock by was the first thing that calmed her down.

I made outside the best part of her day. Every time she went where I wanted, she got paid immediately, and the treat had to be gentle on a senior stomach because the last thing I needed was to upset her digestion while I was training it. I used Jiminy's Soft & Chewy Dog Training Treats in sweet potato and peas, small, soft, easy for older teeth, and made from simple ingredients that didn't send her gut into a tailspin. Those treats turned the yard into a place worth going, and within about ten days she was heading for the door on her own instead of circling the living room.

The other half of the fix was giving her anxiety somewhere to go so it stopped landing in a pile on my carpet. An under-stimulated, bored, stressed dog has a lower tolerance for everything, and she needed a legal outlet for the chewing and gnawing that nervous dogs do. I got her Frienhund Tough Dog Toys for Aggressive Chewers, a three-pack built to survive serious jaws. She wasn't a huge dog, but she was a powerful, determined chewer when she was wound up, and every softer toy I'd tried was gutted in minutes. These held up, and giving her something sturdy to work on during my work hours noticeably dropped the pacing, the whining, and the accidents.

I also cleaned every old spot with an enzymatic cleaner so the house stopped advertising "bathroom" from the floor. Combined with the schedule and the outlet for her nerves, the daily messes went from multiple to occasional to gone.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Get a senior dog checked by a vet before you assume it's a training problem, because pain and cognitive decline are real and fixable-in-part, and no schedule on earth beats an untreated medical cause. Then drop the punishment for good, it only teaches hiding. Put her on a schedule you could set your watch by, pay her instantly and gently every time she goes out, give her a job for her teeth and her nerves, and clean old spots with enzymes.

Set your expectations for weeks, not days. My ten-year-old took about a month to turn the corner, and I nearly quit at week four. If it had gone on longer, I would have hired a trainer who understands rescue trauma, there's no shame in asking for help with a hard case. She's been accident-free for three months now. She just needed someone to believe she was doing her best and then make it easier for her to do better.
