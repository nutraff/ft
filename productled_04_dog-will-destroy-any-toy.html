My Dog Will Destroy Any Toy She Can Get Her Mouth On — Then I Tried One She Can't

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

November 15, 2024

I keep a running tally of the casualties. A "tough" rope tug lasted eleven minutes. A so-called indestructible rubber bone, four days, because she took a corner off it and then spent a week slowly unzipping the rest. A plush duck with one squeaker survived all of about ninety seconds before there was a small pile of white fluff on my living room rug and a very satisfied eighty-pound pit mix named Roo looking up at me like she'd done the dishes.

The part that actually scared me wasn't the money, though I was spending real money. It was watching her swallow a chunk of orange rubber the size of my thumb before I could get it out of her mouth. She was fine, but I spent two days staring at her, and I realized I had a problem that wasn't just "she's a chewer." I was buying toys faster than she could destroy them, and none of them were safe.

Why This Happens

Destruction and chewing are not the same instinct, and confusing them is where I went wrong for months. Chewing is normal, self-soothing dog behavior, it releases tension, and for a lot of breeds it's about as instinctive as breathing. A dog who chews well is doing something healthy. The problem starts when the chewing has no legitimate outlet, because that energy has to go somewhere.

There's also a strong genetic component. Breeds developed for gripping, holding, and shaking things, terriers, shepherds, bully-type dogs, tend to be relentless with their mouths. Roo is a mix of two of those, so in hindsight the shredded library of toys was never a mystery. She wasn't punishing me. She was being exactly what she was bred to be, minus a job.

The other force is arousal. A dog who's wound up, from a long day alone, from the mail carrier, from a good romp in the yard. Goes after toys harder. A toy that survives a calm dog at 8 a.m. May not survive that same dog at 6 p.m. When she's still got battery left. And here's the kicker: the harder a dog goes at a toy, the faster she finds the weak point. Most "tough" toys fail at the seams and the squeaker, not the solid body.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

Buying bigger. I figured a toy too big to get her jaws around would last. Instead she clamped down on the end and worked it loose, and a toy that was too heavy for her to carry lost its appeal entirely, she'd ignore it and go back to the couch.

Buying cheap. I'd read that if she destroys everything, you should just give her dollar-store toys. That's the worst advice I got. Cheap toys fail into small, sharp pieces, exactly the kind of thing a dog can choke on or swallow. The stakes went up, not down.

Taking her toys away. When the destruction peaked, I gathered everything up and left her with nothing to chew. Predictable result: she found the coffee table leg. A dog with no legal outlet doesn't stop chewing; she just relocates the behavior to something you care about more.

What Actually Worked

The real fix was two parts, and I had to do both of them. The first part was picking a toy that could actually take the abuse. I started looking past the marketing word "indestructible" and reading the material and thickness. What finally held up for Roo was a solid natural-rubber toy without a hollow squeaker to disembowel, dense enough that she could gnaw rather than puncturing through in one bite. Once she had something that didn't collapse, the whole frantic quality of her chewing changed. She stopped trying to dismember it and started, for the first time, actually settling in to chew it. That's the moment I realized she'd never hated her toys; she'd been frustrated by them failing under her.

The second part was teaching her to trade, and this is where treats did the heavy lifting. I'd give her the durable chew and let her get busy. Then I'd say "drop it," hold a treat at my nose, and the instant she let go, the treat appeared. Buddy Biscuits Trainers Training Bites were perfect for this because they're soft enough to break into tiny pieces, so I could repeat the sequence twenty times in five minutes without stuffing her. Once she understood that releasing her prize reliably paid, she stopped guarding the chew and stopped the frantic clamp-down that was wrecking everything. I kept a second bag of Jiminy's Soft & Chewy Dog Training Treats, sweet potato and peas, for the longer sessions, and the smaller, plant-forward pieces kept her from filling up before the training was done.

I also built in a legitimate outlet so she had something to do besides demolish things. A ten-minute session of tug-and-release, plus a stuffed rubber toy she only got when I left, took the edge off the boredom that was fueling the worst of it. A tired, satisfied mouth stops looking for furniture.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Stop shopping by the word "indestructible" and start looking at what the toy is made of and how thick it is. If your dog is a serious chewer, a hollow squeaker is a countdown timer. Buy fewer toys, buy better ones, and inspect them every week, the moment you see a chunk missing or a seam splitting, it goes in the trash, because a swallowed piece is a vet bill at best and a surgery at worst.

Then spend five minutes a day on "drop it." It's the single most useful thing I ever taught Roo, and it means I can take anything out of her mouth without a wrestling match. She still chews hard, that's who she is, but now she chews things that survive her, and the trash can full of toy carcasses has finally stopped filling up.
