My Bull Terrier Ate His Way Out of the Crate Every Day — Here's What Finally Worked

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

March 10, 2026

I came home from a nine-hour shift, opened the door, and found a trail of shredded bedding running from the crate to the window. Bruno, my three-year-old bull terrier, had been at it again: the metal tray was pushed halfway across the room, the plastic pan was cracked in two places, and there was a fresh crater dug into the hardwood by the front door where he'd tried to tunnel his way out. He greeted me like nothing had happened, tail going like a windshield wiper, with a chunk of foam still stuck to his lip.

The chewing was the part that scared me. This wasn't a puppy exploring his crate, he was eating plastic and metal, and I'd already paid one emergency bill for a fragment that showed up on an X-ray. The digging was the other half of the problem. He would rake at the crate floor until the pan split, then go to work on the seam of the door. I was buying crates faster than I could afford them, and my neighbors had started texting me about the barking that kicked off the second I pulled out of the driveway.

Why This Happens

Crate chewing and digging in a dog this age almost always trace back to separation anxiety, not mischief or revenge. A dog left alone isn't plotting anything. He's in genuine distress, and the crate becomes the one thing standing between him and the rest of the house, which he associates with his people. The destruction isn't random; it's a frantic attempt to reunite with the pack. The digging especially is a dog pawing at the thing that separates him from you.

Bull terriers make this harder because they're a high-drive breed built for long stretches of activity and constant human company, and they have the jaws and stubbornness to make short work of what a softer breed would quit on. A bored terrier who can't self-soothe is a demolition crew with a heartbeat.

There's also a comfort angle most people miss. A crate that's a little too small, has a rough floor, or sits in a drafty or noisy spot never lets the dog fully relax. And separation anxiety tends to peak in the first thirty to forty-five minutes after you leave, which is exactly the window when the damage happens. A vet will usually want to rule out a medical cause and then separate true separation anxiety from confinement anxiety, because those two need different fixes.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first move was a bigger crate. I upgraded to an extra-large wire model, figuring the old one felt cramped and that alone was the problem. It wasn't. The panic had nothing to do with space, he still dug at the floor and chewed the bars the moment I walked out.

Then I tried leaving him loose in the kitchen behind a baby gate, thinking the freedom would calm him down. It backfired badly. Without the crate, he tore at the door frame, pulled the trash apart, and scratched the paint down to bare drywall. Confinement was failing; freedom was failing harder.

Third, I leaned on food. A frozen stuffed toy and a peanut-butter Kong bought me maybe twenty minutes of quiet. The instant the food was gone, the digging and chewing restarted, because the underlying feeling, I'm alone and I can't stand it, hadn't changed at all.

What Actually Worked

What finally moved the needle was treating the anxiety instead of the symptoms, starting with a ThunderShirt for Dogs Calming Anxiety Vest. It wraps the torso in gentle, constant pressure, the same idea behind swaddling a baby or using a weighted blanket. And for a dog in a spiral that pressure is surprisingly grounding. I slipped it on about ten minutes before I left so it registered as normal rather than as a signal that I was about to abandon him, and the frantic first half hour started shrinking. He still didn't love being alone, but he stopped trying to eat his way out of the door.

The second piece was a Hemp Oil for Dogs and Cats worked into a daily routine. Used consistently rather than as a one-off on bad days, it helped smooth his baseline, that low-grade vigilance where he spent all day listening for my car. I won't pretend it sedates a dog; it doesn't. But stacked with the vest, it raised his threshold so the small noises that used to tip him into a full meltdown stopped doing it.

The rest was unglamorous structure. I practiced the departure in tiny pieces, pick up the keys, walk to the door, come back before he escalated. Until the whole ritual stopped meaning abandonment. I kept hellos and goodbyes flat and boring. I gave him a real workout before I left, and I set out a food-stuffed toy and a snuffle-style puzzle so the first hour had a job to do. I also put up a camera, and that's what taught me the truth: the meltdown peaked in the first thirty minutes and faded once he finally crashed. Seeing that let me stop panicking every time I left.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Get a camera before you buy anything. You can't fix a problem you're only guessing at from the evidence on the floor, and the footage tells you whether you're dealing with separation anxiety or just a bored chewer. Two very different problems. Then set up your departures so they're short, boring, and repeated a dozen times a day at first, until leaving stops being an event your dog braces for.

If your dog is hurting himself, bloody paws from digging, cracked teeth, anything he swallowed, that is not a training problem. That's a call to your vet or a veterinary behaviorist, and there's no shame in it. It's the same as seeing a specialist for a bad knee. For us, the combination of the vest, the daily hemp oil, and a departure routine that stopped feeling like a goodbye did what three crates never could. Six months in, Bruno sleeps through my shifts. I still find the occasional foam crumb, but the door is intact. And so is he.
