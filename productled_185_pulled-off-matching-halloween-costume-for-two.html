How I Pulled Off a Matching Halloween Costume for Two Pets Who Both Hate Clothes

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

August 13, 2026

Last October I decided my dachshund, Waffle, and my rabbit, Poppy, would go trick-or-treating as a hot dog and its bun. In my head it was adorable. In reality, forty minutes before the neighborhood party, I was kneeling on the kitchen floor with a hot glue gun, one pet hiding under the couch, and the other wearing half a costume and looking at me like I had betrayed him personally.

Duo costumes for two pets are a special kind of chaos, because now you have two opinions about clothing, two body shapes that don't match any pattern, and one of you. But I got there, the photos were genuinely great, and nobody lost an eye. It just took a totally different approach than the one I started with.

Why This Happens

Pets hate costumes for sensory reasons, not dramatic ones. Clothing changes how they feel their own body, muffles their hearing, tickles their back, and often blocks their vision. And any one of those can be enough to make a pet freeze, tuck tail, or bolt. A dog who trusts you will still paw at a hat that won't stay on. A rabbit, who is prey to everything, will take one whiff of a strange-fitting fabric and simply refuse to move.

Two pets multiplies every problem. Now you're matching a theme across two completely different silhouettes, a long low body and a round fluffy one, while also keeping both animals comfortable and both costumes in one visual story. Most store-bought "matching" sets are made for two dogs of similar size, which is why a dachshund and a rabbit need a plan, not a package.

And safety underlies all of it. Halloween costumes can snag on furniture, choke on loose neck straps, overheat an animal, or hide a pet from you right when a crowd is pressing in. The cuter the idea, the more it tempts you to skip the part where you check that the dog can breathe, see, hear, and walk normally in the thing.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

First I ordered a "matching pair" costume set online. It arrived sized for two medium dogs, so it gaped off Waffle's chest and swallowed Poppy completely. Lesson one: measure both pets and treat each one as its own fit, no matter what the listing promises.

Then I went full DIY with felt and a glue gun the night before. It looked fine for the first photo and came apart by the third sniff, one strap snapped when Waffle shook, and a glued seam peeled off Poppy's costume within minutes. Last-minute crafting plus a shedding dachshund plus glue equals a mess I had to clean off a rabbit.

My worst attempt was bribery, but way too late. I tried to get them used to the costumes five minutes before we left, stuffing treats into their mouths while wrestling elastic over their heads. All that did was teach them that treats now meant something unpleasant was coming. By the time we needed them calm and dressed, they were already wary of me.

What Actually Worked

The fix started a week early. I picked a theme that worked with their actual shapes instead of against them, Waffle's long body made the "hot dog" half easy, and Poppy's round shape became the bun sitting behind him. Choosing a concept that leaned into what they already look like did more for the final photo than any amount of engineering.

Then I desensitized slowly. Every day for a few days I put the costume on the floor with treats around it, then draped it loosely over each pet for a few seconds and rewarded, building up to wearing it for a full minute. Short, boring sessions beat one big dramatic dress rehearsal. Waffle stopped pawing at the bun within three days, and Poppy, who I expected to be impossible, tolerated the round bun far better than the tight store version because it was soft and open at the bottom.

For the build itself I used lightweight, adjustable pieces and skipped anything around the neck. I measured each pet with a soft tape, chose a soft, stretchy base that moved with them, and kept the costume off their heads entirely so nobody's vision or hearing was blocked. Nothing hanging low enough to snag, nothing tight enough to pinch. I test-walked each pet up and down the hallway to prove they could move and see before I ever called it done.

The unglamorous backup that saved the night was a first-aid kit. Any time you're draping fabric and elastic on pets in a crowded, exciting setting, you want to be ready for a scraped pad, a snagged nail, or a costume that rubs a raw spot under the leg. Having a proper pet first-aid kit within reach meant that when Waffle's leg strap chafed after an hour, I cleaned and covered it in two minutes instead of cutting the night short. It is the least photogenic purchase I made all October and the one I was gladdest to own.

Finally, I planned a tight photo window. Pets don't care that you want ninety candids. I put the costumes on, took the shots in about fifteen minutes while everyone was still relaxed, and took them off again before either pet hit their breaking point. Treats before, during, and after, in tiny pieces, kept it a good deal for them instead of a hostage situation.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Comfort beats cuteness every time. Pick a theme that fits the bodies you actually have, fit each pet separately, and start desensitizing a week out with slow, treat-heavy, boring sessions. Skip anything tight around the neck, anything that blocks eyes or ears, and anything dangling that can catch on furniture.

And have a plan B ready, because a naked pet is a perfectly good pet. If either animal is stressed, shivering, or trying to hide, take the costume off and call the duo a solo act. The best Halloween photos I have are the ones where both pets look relaxed, and that only happened once I stopped forcing the costume and started earning it.
