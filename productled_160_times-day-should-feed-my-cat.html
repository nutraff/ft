How Many Times a Day Should I Feed My Cat? What Finally Worked for Us

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

May 18, 2026

For the first four years I had my cat, I never really fed her. I filled a bowl and she ate when she felt like it, and I called that being a good owner. Then the vet said the word "overweight," and I looked down at a cat who'd developed a little swaying belly and a habit of screaming at me from the kitchen every time I stood up. Somehow, without ever once being "fed," she'd gained two and a half pounds.

So I did the thing every confused owner does and started Googling. Feed twice a day. Feed three times. Free-feed dry and give wet as a treat. Cats should graze. Cats should never graze. Every source contradicted the last, and none of them told me the actual number I needed, which was grams per day. That's when I stopped reading and started measuring.

Why This Happens

Cats are naturally small-meal hunters. A wild cat eats several mouse-sized meals a day, each one a hunt, not two giant buffets. That's why a cat left with an always-full bowl grazes all day in tiny nibbles and, in many households, takes in more calories than anyone realizes. Free-feeding works for the rare cat who self-regulates. For most cats, especially indoor ones who don't burn much energy, it's a slow weight gain hiding in plain sight.

Here's the piece that finally made it click: a cat's daily calorie need is tied to ideal weight and body condition, not the weight on the scale. A ten-pound cat and a fourteen-pound cat of the same frame don't need the same amount of food, and the overweight one needs fewer calories than she'd need at her healthy weight. Feed to the body you want, not the body you have, that's the line my vet used, and it reframed the whole problem for me.

And the reason my cat was screaming at me wasn't hunger, at least not the way I assumed. Free-fed cats rarely get truly hungry, but they do get bored, and a kitchen that produces food randomly is a kitchen worth yelling at. Once I put her on a schedule, the screaming actually got better, because she learned when the meal was coming instead of hoping it was every time I walked past.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first attempt was to just feed her "less", scoop a bit less into the bowl and hope. That failed immediately because a scoop isn't a quantity. My scoops varied by the day, the food settled differently each time, and I had no idea what I'd actually fed her from one week to the next. You can't reduce a number you never measured.

Then I went the other way and tried one big meal a day, thinking one clean feeding would be easier to control. She ate it all in five minutes, threw up bile on the carpet around 4 a.m. From the long empty stretch, and spent the evening yowling at the bowl. Once-a-day feeding is hard on a cat's digestive rhythm and a disaster for behavior.

Next I tried a strict crash reduction, cut her food hard to get the weight off fast. She lost a little, then started stealing the dog's food and knocking the trash over. Too aggressive a cut is worse than no cut, because a cat that's suddenly starving will find calories somewhere, and severe restriction isn't safe for cats anyway. Slow and measured is the only safe direction.

What Actually Worked

The change that stuck was boring and precise: I figured out a daily calorie target with my vet, converted it into grams of her specific food, and measured every meal on a kitchen scale. Not a cup, not a scoop. Grams. A cheap digital scale ended the guessing in a single afternoon, and once I knew that she got, say, forty grams a day split into meals, everything else fell into place.

Then I split that daily total into meals. Two measured meals worked for my cat's age and activity, with the option of a small third if she got demanding, and I kept them at the same times every day so her gut and her brain could both predict them. Scheduled meals plus measured portions is the whole system. The schedule fixes the behavior; the measurement fixes the weight.

The next upgrade was mechanical, and it changed how much of my life the cat controlled. I added an automatic portion-control feeder that drops a set amount at set times, which meant I stopped being the human who dispenses food and started being the human who shows up for pets and play. Two things happened: my cat stopped begging at me specifically, and I could travel overnight without a cat-sitter managing a precise diet. If your cat wakes you at dawn for breakfast, this is the single best fix there is.

I kept a slow-feed bowl for her wet-food meal, too, so that portion took real effort to finish instead of disappearing in seconds, and I logged her weight once a week on the same scale. Weighing weekly is how you catch a slide early instead of at the annual visit when it's four pounds of problem. Nine months in, she was down to a healthy weight, and the begging had mostly stopped because the schedule did the negotiating for me.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Get a calorie target from your vet, buy a kitchen scale, and measure in grams, not scoops. Split the daily total into two or three scheduled meals, and let an automatic feeder handle the timing if your mornings matter to you. Weigh your cat weekly and write it down.

Two meals a day is a fine, normal answer for most adult cats. The number that matters isn't how many times, it's how much. Measure the amount, keep the schedule steady, and your cat will settle into the routine faster than you'd believe.
