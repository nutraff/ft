My Senior Dog's Bloodwork Was Fine — but She Was Stiff. Here's What Actually Helped Her Move

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

July 29, 2025

Every year I take my eleven-year-old Labrador, Sadie, in for her senior wellness visit, and every year I brace for the bloodwork. Last year it came back boring, which is the best possible result, liver, kidneys, thyroid, all where they should be. And yet she was not herself. She was slow getting up off the hardwood. She hesitated at the bottom of the stairs. She stopped jumping onto the couch, and then she stopped even trying. The numbers said she was fine; my eyes said she was stiff and sore, and it turned out both things were true at once.

That is the confusing part about older dogs. Bloodwork catches a lot of things, but it does not catch the wear that happens in joints. So I spent a few months assuming she was "just getting old," which is a thing people say when they do not want to deal with what is actually a manageable problem.

Why This Happens

Arthritis in dogs is not a disease of old age so much as a disease of accumulated wear. The cartilage that cushions a joint is smooth and slippery when a dog is young, and over years of running, jumping, and landing, it gradually breaks down. Once that cushion thins, bone starts rubbing on bone, and the joint gets inflamed, stiff, and painful. Large breeds and dogs who had an active life, a Labrador who spent a decade fetching, tend to show it earlier and harder, because their joints took more of a beating.

The reason owners miss it is that dogs are wired to hide pain. A dog in early joint discomfort does not limp dramatically or cry. It just gets a little slower, a little more reluctant, a little less interested in the things it used to love, and we read that as aging. By the time a dog is visibly limping consistently, the arthritis is usually well established. The stiffness I saw in Sadie was her coping, not her complaining.

The other piece is that mobility is a whole-body issue, not just one joint. A dog with sore hips shifts its weight forward, which overloads the shoulders; a dog with a sore knee limps, which strains the opposite leg. And the less a dog moves, the more the muscles around the joints weaken, which makes the joints less supported and the stiffness worse. So the trap is twofold: pain makes a dog move less, and moving less makes the pain worse. Any real fix has to address both the joints and the muscles around them.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first instinct was to let her rest. If she was stiff, surely the answer was fewer walks and more couch time. That was exactly backwards. Cutting her activity made her weaker, and within a couple of weeks she was stiffer than before. Rest is right after an injury; as a long-term strategy for arthritis, it accelerates the decline.

Then I tried a human painkiller from the cabinet for a few days, thinking if it worked for me it would work for her. I stopped almost immediately when I looked it up, because many common human pain meds are dangerous to dogs, some at surprisingly small doses. I want to be clear about this: never give a dog human medication without a vet telling you to. That could have seriously hurt her.

I also bought the cheapest glucosamine supplement I could find online and gave it for a month. Nothing changed, partly because a month is not long enough for a joint supplement to show anything, and partly because I had no idea whether the dose was even meaningful. I was guessing, and guessing wasted a month of her walking comfortably.

What Actually Worked

The foundation was a proper conversation with my vet about joint support and pain management, because there are real options for arthritis and I had been trying to manage a medical problem in my living room. Once that was in place, the everyday routine did the heavy lifting. The core of it was a daily joint support chew with glucosamine and chondroitin, given consistently, these are building blocks that support the cartilage and the joint environment, and the point of them is consistency over months, not a quick fix. I keep Sadie on one every morning like clockwork, because a joint supplement only works if it is in her system every day.

The piece I did not expect to matter as much as it did was her gut. Prompted by her vet, I added a daily probiotic to her routine, thinking of it as general senior support rather than anything to do with her hips. What I noticed over the following months was a dog who seemed more comfortable overall, steadier digestion, better coat, and, I think, less of the general inflammation that comes with an unhappy gut. I use Pet Honesty Dog Probiotics Chews for this; they are a soft chew she takes without a fight, and giving her one alongside her joint chew turned the whole thing into a two-chew morning ritual that takes ten seconds. I am not going to pretend a probiotic is a joint medicine. But supporting a senior dog's overall health, digestion included, is part of keeping her comfortable, and for a dog her age every bit of that adds up.

The rest was physical. I started short, gentle daily walks on soft ground and kept them going even on days she seemed stiff, because movement keeps the muscles supporting the joint. I added ramps and a low step stool so she does not have to jump to get on the couch or into the car, and I put a couple of rug runners on the hardwood so she is not slipping with every step, which is both scary and how older dogs strain something. Slower walks, better footing, and a low-impact routine left her visibly more willing to move. Three months in, she climbs the stairs again, slowly but on her own.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Do not write off stiffness as "just old age." A stiff dog is usually a dog in discomfort, and discomfort is something you can work on. Start with your vet. Arthritis has real treatments, and pain meds for dogs are prescription for good reason. Add a joint support chew and give it every single day for months before you judge it, and support the whole dog, digestion included, because senior health is a system. Then change the environment so your dog is not jumping, slipping, or straining, and keep gentle daily movement going instead of resting the stiffness away.

I will not get more years with Sadie than any of us get with our dogs, but I got her comfortable ones, and the stairs back. That was worth every chew and every rug runner.
