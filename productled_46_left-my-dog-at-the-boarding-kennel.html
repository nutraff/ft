I Left My Dog at the Boarding Kennel for Nine Days. The Camera Was the Only Thing That Let Me Sleep.

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

April 12, 2025

The farthest I had ever been from my dog was one overnight at a friend's house. Then my partner and I booked a nine-day trip we had planned for a year, and the only option that made sense was boarding. Scout is a seven-year-old rescue, a shepherd mix, and the most attached dog I have ever had — he follows me room to room. Handing his leash to a stranger at the kennel door and walking away was, honestly, one of the worst ten minutes of my year.

The guilt I could handle. It was the not-knowing that undid me. Was he eating? Was he the dog cowering in the back of a run, or the one getting belly rubs? I lay awake at 2 a.m. Running worst-case scenarios with zero information to stop them. That is the specific torture of boarding: you hand your dog to people you barely know and then you are cut off, and your imagination fills the silence with every terrible thing it can invent.

Why This Happens

Dogs cannot text you, and kennel staff are busy. Those two facts are the whole problem. A boarding facility might have fifty dogs, a handful of staff, and a front desk fielding calls all day. When you phone to check in, the person who answers is doing their best, but they will tell you what they can see in ten seconds — he's doing fine — because that is genuinely all they have time to observe about your specific dog. It is not neglect. It is arithmetic.

Meanwhile the owner brain is built to worry. Attachment runs both directions, and being physically separated with no channel of information flips normal concern into overdrive. Every unanswered question balloons into a catastrophe in your head, and the uncertainty itself, not any actual problem, is what ruins the trip.

There is also a practical reason a camera beats a phone call: you catch things a report never would. Whether your dog is really eating, whether he is lying comfortably or pacing in circles, whether he has picked up a limp, whether he has shut down in a corner and gone quiet. Seeing is simply better information than hearing, and it is the difference between guessing and knowing.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

First I called the front desk every morning. The reports were kind and completely generic: he's good, he ate. I appreciated the effort, but it did nothing for an owner who wanted to know whether he was stressed. He's good and he's shut down sound identical over the phone.

Then I asked the staff to text me a photo once a day. Day one, I got a blurry shot of a dog I was about 80 percent sure was Scout. Day two, nothing — the person working my shift had never gotten the message. Day three, another photo, hours late. Relying on a human relay through a busy kennel is unreliable, and I do not blame them; I just needed something that did not depend on someone remembering.

My last attempt was the desperate one: I drove forty minutes to the facility and visited mid-stay. Scout was thrilled for about five minutes and then agitated for an hour after I left. The visit upset him more than it reassured me, and the staff confirmed his next day was rougher. I had made it worse for both of us. That is when I stopped looking for ways to physically be there and started looking for a window.

What Actually Worked

The thing that fixed it was pointing a camera at the question. Boarding facilities have gotten smart about this, and more of them now offer their own webcam feed or at least welcome a camera for the dogs. When I started calling around for our next trips, camera access moved to the top of my checklist, above price. A facility with a live feed where I can open an app and see Scout's run at any hour, from anywhere, removes most of the anxiety. I check in once in the morning and once at night, deliberately not more, and I sleep.

If you use an in-home sitter or a friend instead — which is what we do for most trips now — the setup is even easier, because you bring your own camera. A pet camera with a phone app that does live video, two-way audio, and night vision sits on a counter pointed at your dog's favorite spot, and suddenly you are not guessing. I can see he is sprawled on the couch, I can talk to him through the speaker, and the motion alerts mean I would know right away if something were wrong instead of after the fact. That is the feature that matters: a live view and notifications that reach you if your dog has been barking for an hour.

One small logistics note turned out to matter after every stay. Dogs often come home from boarding and temporarily forget their routines, and house-training signals can wobble for a few days. We kept a wireless dog doorbell by the back door so Scout could tell us he needed out without me hovering and second-guessing him, and it got the routine back on track fast. It is not why the camera worked, but it is the kind of small tool that smooths the rough week after a trip.

The other change was mental: I set rules for myself about checking. Live video is a gift and also a trap — refresh every ten minutes and you will invent stress the camera never shows. Twice a day, plus alerts. That is it. Bounded checking gave me the reassurance without letting it become its own problem.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Ask about camera access before you book, and be ready to pay a little more for it — for me it is worth more than any other feature a kennel offers. If you board at home with a sitter, buy a camera with night vision, two-way audio, and motion alerts, and test it before you leave. Then set your checking schedule and stick to it, because the schedule is what keeps the tool from turning into a new source of anxiety.

Your dog is not sitting in a run feeling abandoned forever; he is living his day, and most of the time the camera will prove he is fine. Being able to see that is what let me actually enjoy the trip. We came home to a dog who was healthy, a little tired, and very happy to see us, and I already knew all of that before I walked in the door.
