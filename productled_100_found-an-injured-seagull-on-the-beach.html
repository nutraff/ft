I Found an Injured Seagull on the Beach — Here's the Right Way to Help (and What Not to Do)

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

October 19, 2025

It was a late afternoon on the beach when I noticed a seagull that did not fly away as I walked past. That was the first red flag, because healthy gulls either stand their ground and squawk or they leave; they do not sit motionless while a person approaches. This one was hunched, one wing drooping at an odd angle with what looked like monofilament fishing line wrapped around it, and it was watching me with one eye and clearly not going anywhere.

What I learned over the next few hours is that rescuing a wild bird is not about heroics. It is about containing the situation safely and getting the animal to people who actually know what they are doing. I had no kit and no plan, and I improvised. Doing this again, I would have three things ready, and I would know exactly what not to do.

Why This Happens

Injured wild birds are usually the product of human infrastructure. Fishing line and hooks tangle wings and legs, cars and windows cause collisions, and cats and dogs cause bite wounds, and an animal that survives the initial injury often dies later from shock, dehydration, or infection if it is not handled properly. The reason the right technique matters so much is that handling is itself dangerous to the bird. A wild bird's first response to capture is extreme stress, and stress can kill a bird that might otherwise recover, which is why every step of a rescue should be geared toward keeping the animal as calm, dark, and still as possible.

The other reason people get it wrong is that our instincts are made for pets, not wildlife. We want to hold the animal, comfort it, feed it, give it water, and set it free when it looks better. Almost none of that is right for a wild bird. You should not feed it, because the wrong food or forcing food can cause aspiration and harm; you should not give it water, because an injured or stressed bird can easily inhale it into its lungs; you should not release it just because it is flapping, because a bird that cannot fly properly will not survive; and you should not handle it more than absolutely necessary. The goal is a safe transfer, not treatment.

Then there is the practical danger to you. Seagulls and other large birds have strong beaks and will bite and stab when frightened, and their feet and wings can thrash in ways that hurt both of you. A bird with a wound can carry infection, and a bird tangled in line or hooks can cut you when you grab it.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first instinct was to grab the gull with my bare hands and carry it, and I got one gnarled beak jab to the thumb for it. That approach risks your safety and the bird's, a large, frightened bird can injure itself badly thrashing against your grip. Bare-handed pickup of a big wild bird is a bad idea on every level.

Then I thought about wrapping it in whatever I had and driving it to my house, planning to "keep it comfortable" overnight and figure out help in the morning. That is a classic mistake. Without the right container and proper care, a night in a human house is more stress and exposure than the bird can usually take, and delay costs animals their lives.

The third thing I nearly did was offer it a piece of bread and some water from my bottle, because it looked exhausted and it felt cruel not to. I am glad I stopped. Bread and other human food are the wrong diet and can do real harm, and water offered to a stressed injured bird is a choking and drowning risk. Feeding and watering wild rescue birds is almost always the wrong call. The rehabilitator handles that, properly, if the bird makes it that far.

What Actually Worked

The approach that actually works starts before you touch the bird, with a safe container. What the bird needs is a dark, ventilated, quiet box, a sturdy cardboard box with air holes is ideal, because darkness reduces stress and a closed container prevents the bird from thrashing or escaping while you transport it. You want gloves so your hands and the bird are protected from each other, and a towel or blanket to drape over the bird and gently scoop it up. This is where a purpose-built wild bird rescue kit earns its keep: a proper kit gives you a ventilated carrier, gloves, and a towel sized for the job, so you are not improvising containment on the sand with your bare hands.

The technique itself is calm and deliberate. I draped the towel over the seagull to cover its head and body, then gathered the towel around the bird with both hands, keeping the wings folded against the body, and moved it into the box in one smooth motion rather than a grab. Then I closed the box, kept it dark and still, and did not open it again or fuss with it. No food, no water, no handling. The bird sat quietly in the dark, which is exactly the state you want it in.

Then came the part that matters most, which is getting it to the right people. I called a local wildlife rehabilitation center and followed their instructions, and I would tell anyone to have that number ready before you need it. Rehabbers are the ones who can properly assess and treat the bird and either release it or give it a life that works. My job was not to fix the seagull. My job was to keep it alive and untouched long enough to hand it to someone who knew how. The bird did make it to a rehabber, the line came off clean, and I was told it was released a few weeks later (which is the outcome you are aiming for, and it came from doing less, not more.)

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

If you find an injured wild bird, do not grab it bare-handed, do not take it home overnight, and do not feed it or give it water. Instead, cover it gently with a towel, gather it up with the wings held against the body, and place it in a dark, ventilated box or carrier. Keep it quiet, still, and warm, do not handle it again, and call a licensed wildlife rehabilitator right away and follow what they tell you. Wear gloves, keep the bird away from your face and your pets, and remember that the best thing you can do for a wild animal is almost always less touching and faster transport, not more care at home.

I have a rescue kit in my car now, and a rehabilitation center saved in my phone. The seagull did not need me to be a hero. It needed me to know what not to do.
