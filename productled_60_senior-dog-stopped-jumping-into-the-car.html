My Senior Dog Stopped Jumping Into the Car. Here's What Brought Him Back.

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

May 30, 2025

My lab Scout is eleven, and for most of his life he launched into the back of my SUV in one easy bound. Then one day he stopped. He stood at the tailgate, looked up, and just waited. I lifted his front paws to help and he let me, which he had never done. That was the day I stopped pretending he was still a young dog and started doing something about it.

It was not sudden — looking back, the signs had been there for months. He was slower on the stairs. He took a while to rise off the floor in the morning. He stopped jumping onto the couch. I had chalked all of it up to him just getting older, which is half true and half an excuse to do nothing. When your dog stops doing something he used to love, that is a signal, not a personality change.

Why This Happens

Dogs age a lot like we do, and the biggest changes show up in the joints. Arthritis and general joint stiffness are extremely common in senior dogs, especially larger breeds, and the wear on cartilage that accumulates over a lifetime eventually makes movement painful. A dog who used to bound into the car does not stop because he lost interest — he stops because it hurts.

The muscle angle matters too. A senior dog who moves less loses muscle, and less muscle means less support for the joints, which means more pain, which means even less movement. That spiral is the thing to interrupt, and you interrupt it with a combination of pain management and joint support, not one or the other alone.

Digestion is the piece I did not expect. Older dogs often develop a more sensitive gut, and a senior dog on any new supplement, or with a changing appetite, can benefit from gut support. A dog who is not digesting well is not absorbing nutrients well, which matters more, not less, as he ages. So the plan my vet and I built covered both the joints and the gut.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first instinct was to let him rest more, which turned out to be exactly wrong. Less movement stiffened him up and he got worse, not better. A senior dog needs appropriate, gentle activity to keep the joints moving and the muscles working.

I briefly considered giving him something from my own medicine cabinet, which is a terrible idea and one a shocking number of people try. Human pain medications can be toxic to dogs, and giving a dog an NSAID that was not prescribed for him can cause serious harm. I asked my vet instead, which is the only correct move.

I tried a cheap glucosamine tablet from a big-box store. Scout barely ate it and I saw no change, and a supplement your dog will not take helps nobody. So I went back and did it properly, with the vet.

What Actually Worked

The real change came from a proper joint supplement, chosen with my vet and given consistently. I used the Woyamay Joint Supplement for Dogs, a glucosamine-based soft chew formulated for joint support. What mattered was consistency and format: a soft chew he eats without a fight, given every single day rather than sporadically. Over about six weeks I noticed him rising more easily in the morning and moving with less hesitation on the stairs. It is not a magic cure and it did not make him young again, but it clearly made movement less daunting, and along with his vet's plan he started asking to get on the couch again.

Alongside the joints, I addressed his gut, which I had not thought about at all. I added Pet Honesty Dog Probiotics Chews for his digestive health, and it turned out to be a quiet win. Senior dogs often deal with softer stools and a more fragile digestive system, and the probiotics firmed him up and settled his stomach. A dog who feels well in his gut eats better, and a dog who eats well holds his weight and his muscle, which loops right back to joint support.

The third piece was movement management. Instead of letting him rest, I gave him short, gentle daily walks on flat ground, and I stopped asking him to do the things that hurt — no more jumping into the car, which I replaced by lifting him or using a ramp. Exercise keeps the joints lubricated and the muscles up, but it has to be the right kind and amount. I watched, adjusted, and backed off on bad days.

The last thing was weight. Every extra pound is extra strain on aging joints, and keeping Scout lean was one of the highest-value things I could do for him. I measured his food, cut back on table scraps, and kept him moving gently. He lost a couple of pounds, and it showed in how he moved.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

If your senior dog is slowing down, take it seriously instead of writing it off to age. See your vet, because pain management and joint support together do far more than either alone, and never give a dog human pain medicine. Then build a daily routine: a joint supplement he will actually eat, gut support if his digestion is off, gentle regular movement, and a healthy weight.

Scout is not the dog he was at five, and that is fine. He is comfortable, he is interested, and last week, for the first time in a year, he jumped up onto the couch by himself. I will take every one of those small wins.
