My Five-Month Lab Mix Had Diarrhea and Was Biting Like a Shark — What Was Actually Going On

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

February 5, 2026

Two problems, one puppy. That was the summer: my five-month-old lab mix had loose, foul stools every few hours, and at the same time his mouth had apparently become a weapon. He was biting my hands, my ankles, my sleeves, harder and more constantly than he ever had as a tiny pup, and he was pacing and whining like he couldn't get comfortable in his own skin. I was up all night with the diarrhea and dodging his teeth all day, and I kept treating the two things as unrelated.

They weren't unrelated. And I learned that lesson the expensive way, after several weeks of guessing and one very thorough vet visit.

Why This Happens

Start with the diarrhea, because that's the part with a real medical answer. Persistent loose stool in a young puppy is a strong flag for intestinal parasites, and giardia is one of the most common culprits. It's a microscopic parasite that lives in water and damp soil, puddles, streams, the park, even the shared water bowl at the dog run. And puppies pick it up constantly because they put everything in their mouths. It causes episodic, sometimes sulfur-smelling diarrhea and can be miserable to shake without proper treatment.

The biting piece has its own explanation, and it's partly just age. Five months is peak teething and peak puppy mouthiness, with sore gums and an enormous need to chew on something. But here's the connection I missed for weeks: a puppy whose gut is inflamed and uncomfortable is a puppy under stress, and stress does not make dogs calmer, it makes them more reactive, more restless, and quicker to mouth. His digestion and his behavior were being driven by the same underlying discomfort.

Add the adolescent energy of a big mixed-breed puppy who wasn't sleeping well, and you get exactly what I had: a wired, uncomfortable dog who bit because he was overtired and couldn't hold still. The diarrhea and the biting were two symptoms of one rough summer.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first move was to blame the food and change his kibble. I switched brands twice in a month, added a topper, and generally made his digestion more complicated without ever testing for what was actually wrong. Food changes can absolutely cause upset, but they weren't the cause here (and every new food reset his gut and made the run worse.)

Then I tried to burn off the biting with more exercise. I walked him longer and played harder, thinking a tired puppy wouldn't bite. That was backwards. More activity on an unsettled gut and a sleepless dog just pushed him further past his limit, and the evening witching-hour biting got sharper, not softer.

Third, I yelped and scolded every time his teeth hit my skin. Classic advice, terrible fit here. The yelp wound him up like a squeaky toy, and my scolding added stress to a dog whose body was already stressed by whatever was living in his intestines. I was correcting the symptom of a medical problem.

What Actually Worked

The single most important thing I did was get him to the vet and stop guessing. A fecal test confirmed the parasite, and we started the prescribed treatment, which, for a puppy with a growing body and a sensitive stomach, meant following the dosing exactly and going back for a retest to make sure it was actually cleared rather than just quieted. That's the piece people skip, and skipping it is how you end up in a loop of recurrence.

While the medication did its work, I fixed the environmental sources too. Fresh, clean water in a washed bowl every day instead of a shared one. No more drinking from puddles, streams, or the dog-park trough. The parasite survives in damp soil, so I kept him out of standing water, picked up after him immediately, and washed my hands, because giardia can pass to people too. I kept him on a simple, bland diet until his stool firmed up, then transitioned back slowly, over about a week, rather than all at once.

For the biting, once his gut settled, the fix got much easier. I went back to the basics that had been drowned out: structured rest so he wasn't overtired, short training sessions instead of endless play, and ending every mouthy episode by calmly withdrawing attention so teeth meant the fun stopped. A puppy whose body feels good is a puppy who can actually learn.

I also gave his nervous system a hand when he was overstimulated. My vet suggested a pressure-style calming wrap, and I got the ThunderShirt for Dogs Calming Anxiety Vest in medium heather gray. I was skeptical, but the gentle, constant pressure genuinely helped him settle during the high-energy evenings and made trips to the vet far less of a wrestling match, which, given how many vet visits we had that summer, was worth it by itself. A calmer puppy is a puppy who bites less, and this gave him a way down when he couldn't find one on his own.

Finally, the household had a second casualty during all of this: my cat, who hated the commotion, the vet smell, and the newly wired puppy, and who started hiding under the bed and swatting at everyone. I set her up with a jar of RYERCAT Silvervine Sticks, a natural cat chew and play toy, which gave her a private, positive thing to do and let her reclaim some peace on her own terms. Untangling a stressed cat from a stressed dog made the whole house calmer.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

If your puppy has ongoing diarrhea, stop guessing about food and get a fecal test, parasites are common, treatable, and impossible to spot from the outside. Follow the treatment all the way through the retest, control the water sources, and keep your own hands clean, because some of these bugs are zoonotic. Don't confuse the biting with a training failure; an uncomfortable, sleepless puppy bites more, and the behavior usually eases once the body feels better.

And if you've sorted the medical side and the mouthiness is still rough, use rest and calm instead of more stimulation, and don't hesitate to ask your vet about the behavior too. My lab mix is a year and a half now, healthy, and only mildly mouthy when he's excited. That summer, the two problems really were one problem, and the day I stopped treating them separately was the day everything started to get better.
