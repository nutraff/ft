My Rabbit Got Out and Was Gone for Two Hours. Here's What I Changed So It Never Happens Again

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

August 26, 2025

It was my own fault, and I know exactly how it happened. I was carrying groceries in, the screen door didn't latch behind me, and Biscuit, my Holland Lop, all four pounds of him (slipped out under the gap before I even registered he'd moved. I turned around and the yard was empty. For the next two hours I walked the block calling his name like an idiot, because here's the thing nobody tells you about rabbits: when they're scared, they go silent and they hide. Calling a rabbit is like calling a deer.)

I found him wedged under the neighbor's shed, flattened against the dirt, absolutely still. He didn't come when I called. He didn't come when I shook his treat bag, which is the trick that always works indoors. I had to crawl in on my stomach and gather him up, and by the time I got him inside my hands were shaking. Four pounds of rabbit can vanish into a space you'd swear was too small for a mouse.

Why This Happens

Rabbits are prey animals, and their entire survival strategy is built on not being found. Dogs and cats are predators, when they're scared they often run toward something, and many will respond to their name. A scared rabbit does the opposite. It freezes, it flattens, and it finds the tightest, darkest crevice it can reach. That's why calling barely works and why a "lost" rabbit is usually not far at all, just perfectly hidden.

There's a second problem specific to rabbits: they chew. Any harness, tag, or tracker I attach has to survive a rabbit deciding it's a toy, and most gear marketed for dogs is far too heavy for a four-pound body. That's why real GPS trackers for rabbits are such a niche, imperfect thing, the tech that exists is either too bulky, too short-lived, or something the rabbit will work off within a day. I looked, and I decided not to rely on a gadget to save me.

The honest lesson is that the escape is a prevention problem, not a recovery problem. Once a rabbit is out and hiding, you're already behind. Everything that actually mattered happened before Biscuit ever reached the door, and the most useful thing I built afterward was a way to get him to come to me on purpose.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first "solution" was to search more aggressively, moving boxes, poking under furniture. That's a mistake with a frightened rabbit. Every loud movement drives a hiding rabbit deeper and can spook it into a true bolt across a yard, which is how rabbits end up on roads. I got lucky that time, not smart.

Then I tried to make him wear a little harness with an ID tag, thinking at least someone would know whose rabbit it was. He had it off in under two minutes and chewed a hole in the webbing for his trouble. Anything light enough for him to tolerate was light enough for him to dismantle.

I also briefly considered one of those tiny trackers I'd seen for cats, but the battery life and the size didn't add up for a rabbit, and I wasn't going to strap something bulky to him just for peace of mind. I needed a plan that didn't depend on hardware at all.

What Actually Worked

The real fix was teaching Biscuit to come to a sound for a reward, so that if he ever slipped out again, I'd have something better than his name. I got the Left&Right Dog Clicker Training Kit and used the clicker the way you'd train any animal, click, then immediately treat, over and over until the click itself means "food is coming." Once that association was solid, a click meant Biscuit's head came up and he moved toward me, even when he was nervous. It's the same principle food-motivated dog trainers use; it just works on rabbits too, because rabbits are nothing if not food-motivated.

The other half of it was speed of reward. A training cue only holds if the treat arrives instantly, and I kept fumbling zip bags and pockets at exactly the wrong moment. The kissleaf Dog Treat Pouch fixed that, the magnetic closure means I can flip it open and get a treat into my hand with one thumb, and I can clip it to my belt so it's always on me. A pouch sounds trivial until you've watched a treat reward land four seconds late and realized you just taught the animal nothing. The timing is the training.

I paired it in rotation with the Left&Right kit's own silicone treat pouch, which came with the clicker and is soft enough to stuff in a jacket pocket. Having two meant one was always clean and loaded, so I never skipped a session because the pouch was lost under a couch cushion. The training didn't take long, a couple of weeks of short daily sessions, and now a click brings Biscuit out from under the bed and into my lap. And I fixed the actual door, which, honestly, was the cheapest and most important fix of all.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Fix your doors and gaps before you fix anything else. A rabbit can squeeze through a space far smaller than it looks, and a screen door that doesn't latch is the entire ballgame. Once that's done, train a recall cue with a clicker and a pouch of treats, not because it's cute, but because the only thing standing between your rabbit and a busy road is whether it will come when you make a specific sound.

And if your rabbit does get out, don't chase it and don't shout. Go quiet, get low, and sweep the tight dark spaces nearby first, because that's where it will be. Bring the treats and click. If it's been out more than a few minutes and you can't find it, that's when you start knocking on doors, a rabbit that hides well is a rabbit that stays lost unless someone else spots it. Prevention is the whole job here.
