He Was a Bullet in the Backseat. The One Trip That Almost Ended in a Window

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

March 14, 2025

It was a quick trip to the hardware store, the kind I had made a hundred times. Moe rode loose in the back of the SUV, which he loved. Free to pace, to press his nose to a window, to climb between the seats when he felt like it. The window on the passenger side was cracked down because he likes the wind. Then traffic did what traffic does, and the car in front of me slammed to a stop. I braked hard. And Moe, who had been standing up by that open window, became a missile. He shot forward, hit the back of the front seat, spun, and ended up with his shoulder against the glass with the window gap grinning open, four lanes of highway below it, and my heart in my throat.

He was fine. A little shaken, a soft-tissue bruise that resolved in a week. I was not. I drove the rest of the way home at twenty miles an hour with one hand on his chest, and by the time we pulled into the driveway I understood something I had been ignoring for years. Loose in the car is dangerous, for the dog, and for everyone in the car with him. A sixty-pound dog at thirty miles an hour is a projectile with the force of a small piano. The window was just the lucky near-miss out of a dozen possible disasters.

Why This Happens

Every object in a moving car keeps moving when the car stops, and a dog is no exception. A loose dog becomes a projectile in a sudden brake and a battering ram in a turn. Beyond the crash math, a loose dog is a driver distraction, which is one of the things that causes the sudden brake in the first place. And a dog who is free to stand at an open window is one hard bump away from being outside the car entirely.

The other piece is that a dog with the run of the backseat has no settled place, which makes him anxious on the ride and harder to manage once you arrive. A dog who paces the whole trip is a dog who arrives wired, and a wired dog at the other end of the trip is a dog who pulls, jumps, and lunges. The loose backseat does not end at the car door.

The deeper reason this keeps happening is that we think of car containment as a comfort question when it is a safety question. People put the dog in the back and call it handled because it looks fine ninety-nine times out of a hundred. The hundredth time is the one that matters, and it comes with no warning at all.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first reaction was to roll the windows up, which solved the one specific thing that had scared me and nothing else. Moe could still pace, still climb between the seats, still become a projectile. I had locked the door on the only part of the problem I could see and left the rest wide open.

Then I tried just putting him in the open cargo area loose, reasoning that it would be safer than the backseat. It was not. The cargo floor is slick, so now every turn sent him sliding, and he clawed the interior trying to get his footing. He ended up bruised and more frantic than before.

My third attempt was one of those hammock seat covers that clips to the front and back headrests. It helped, it did keep his paws off the seat, but the panels he could reach were not tall or solid enough, and he learned within a week to climb over the side into the front seat. A hammock slows a dog down. It does not contain one.

What Actually Worked

The real fix was to give Moe a defined, solid space he could not launch out of. I used the FXW AuraSpace clear acrylic dog playpen as a barrier across the back of the vehicle, the panels are tall and solid enough that he cannot climb over them the way he climbed the hammock, and the clear acrylic means he can still see forward, which kept him calmer than the dark solid barrier I tried first. He gets a floor to lie on and a wall he cannot scale, and that is the whole ballgame. It also doubles as his containment at the destination, so the same panels that keep him in the back of the car become a safe pen in the back of the pickup or at a campsite.

For trips where he needs to travel on foot in a crowd, the hardware store was for a reason, and now we do the farmers market, the MoNiBloom pet stroller gave me a way to move him safely and contained without a leash-ends-in-chaos situation. It folds down and rides in the back once the playpen is set up, so the two pieces cover the whole trip, highway and sidewalk alike.

And I added one thing that is not a gadget: a harness secured to the seat instead of a collar, for the rides where he does not need the barrier. A collar on a dog who braces forward is the wrong tool; a harness spreads the load and keeps him anchored. The barrier keeps him from becoming a bullet, and the harness keeps him from being a loose cannon on the shorter hops.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Stop letting your dog ride loose. Not because something always happens, but because the one time it does, it happens in a second and you cannot take it back. Give them a defined space with a barrier they cannot climb and a secure point to anchor to, and keep the windows up far enough that a bump cannot become an exit.

Think about the whole trip, not just the highway. The right containment has to work at the far end too, wherever the dog is loose, anxious, or underfoot. Moe rides like a passenger now, not a projectile. He still puts his nose to the wind sometimes, but the glass is between him and the world, and that is a rule I will never break again.
