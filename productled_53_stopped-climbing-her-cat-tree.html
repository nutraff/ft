She'd Stopped Climbing Her Cat Tree. The Flimsy One Was the Problem All Along.

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

May 7, 2025

Cleo's cat tree cost me forty dollars and looked great in the corner for about eight months. Then one evening I watched her launch onto the top platform, the whole tower swayed like a sapling in a breeze, and she came down the same way she went up — fast, annoyed, and done with it. After that she stopped using it entirely. Not gradually. Overnight.

I told myself it was the season, or that she had outgrown it, or that cats are simply fickle. I moved it, cleaned it, sprayed catnip on it. Nothing. For months my beautiful corner cat tree sat there like furniture, while Cleo slept on the back of the couch and used my bookshelf as her lookout. The tree was not broken. It was wobbly, and to a cat that is the same thing.

Why This Happens

Cats handle the world with their paws, and a cat's confidence in the air depends entirely on what her feet tell her. A stable surface under her paws says safe. A surface that shifts, sways, or creaks says not safe, get down. That is not pickiness; it is survival wiring. A cat whose ancestors climbed trees and perched above predators reads an unstable platform as a genuine hazard, and she will abandon it no matter how pretty it looks.

Cheap trees fail here almost by design. To hit a low price, makers use thin particle-board platforms, short posts, and a small, light base. The result is a tower that looks impressive and teeters the moment a ten-pound cat lands on it. The higher the platform, the more the sway is amplified, and the more the cat is punished for doing exactly what the tree is supposed to encourage: climbing.

There is a second issue beyond wobble: size and location. Many cat trees are too small for a grown cat to stretch out on, or placed in a dead corner with nothing to watch. Cats want their vertical real estate near a window, facing the room's action, and they want platforms wide and high enough to actually claim. So even a stable tree can sit unused if it is the wrong shape in the wrong spot. But stability is the first domino. Nothing else matters if the thing shakes.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first attempt was denial: I figured she would rediscover it. I sprinkled catnip on the platforms, and for one glorious afternoon she was all over it before returning to the couch. Catnip bought me a few hours of interest and solved nothing, because the wobble was still there.

Next I tried relocation. I dragged the tree out to the living room, right by the window, where a cat should want to sit. Better light, better view — and she still would not use it, because now the tower swayed in plain sight, and every time she jumped up, the whole thing rocked harder on the wood floor, not less.

Then I got clever and bolted the tree to the wall with a strap at the top. That did reduce the sway, and she started using the lower platform again, which felt like a win. But the base and posts were still flimsy, and the upper levels — the ones she actually wanted — still shuddered on the way up. I had patched the symptom. The tree itself was still the weak point.

What Actually Worked

The thing that fixed it was replacing the tree with one actually built for a grown cat to trust. I looked for three things and ignored everything else: a wide, heavy base, thick posts, and platforms broad enough to lie down on. The difference was immediate. When Cleo jumped onto the new tower, it did not move. No creak, no sway. She stood there, computed that the surface was solid, and — I watched this happen — started stretching out on the top perch like she owned it.

Size and weight are worth paying for, and I stopped shopping by height. A tall tree on a flimsy base is worse than a short one on a solid base, because height multiplies instability. The tree I ended up with is shorter than my old one and heavier by miles, and Cleo uses every single platform. I also used a wall strap on it from day one, because even a good tree can be tipped by a determined launch, and anchoring it costs nothing.

Placement finished the job. I put the sturdy tree back by the window, but now it is oriented so she can climb it and watch both the street and the room at once. Cats claim territory they can survey; giving her that sightline is what turned a perch into her daily station. The old tree's corner had no view, which I now realize was half the reason she ignored it even before it started swaying.

I will be honest that this fix is mostly about spending more than you want to. The heavy-duty tree cost two or three times the flimsy one, and the moment she trusted it, that felt like the best money I had ever spent on her. A cat tree is not decor; it is a piece of climbing equipment. Buy it as if she is going to put her whole weight on it, because she is.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

If your cat ignores her tree, before you blame her, push it. Rock it with your hand. If it moves even a little, that is your answer, and no amount of catnip will out-argue a wobble. Look for a wide, weighted base, thick posts, and platforms big enough to stretch out on — then anchor the top to the wall. Stability beats height, every time.

After that, put it where there is a view and a bit of traffic, not a dead corner. Cleo went from avoiding her tree for months to living in it about a week after the solid one arrived. The tree did not get more interesting. It got trustworthy, and for a cat, that is the whole thing.
