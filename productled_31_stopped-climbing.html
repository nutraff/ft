She Stopped Climbing. I Thought It Was Just Old Age — Until I Measured the Second Step

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

February 18, 2025

Mabel is eleven, a lab mix with gray on her muzzle and a lifelong habit of sleeping on my bed, which she reached by climbing onto an ottoman and then up onto the mattress. It was her nightly ritual for eight years. Then the climbing stopped, and so did she, one day she just stood at the foot of the bed, looked up at the ottoman, and lay down on the floor instead. I said the thing everyone says. She is getting old. She is slowing down. I let it go for almost a month, which I regret.

What broke the spell was a tape measure. I measured the height of that ottoman, fourteen inches, and then the height of the second step of the staircase, where she would also stop and stare. She could manage the first step. It was always the second one she balked at, once she had to shift her weight and push upward with the rear legs. That was the tell. A dog slowed by old age moves slowly everywhere. A dog refusing the second step is a dog telling you something hurts.

Why This Happens

Dogs are built to hide pain, and they are good at it. The hesitation you see on stairs or when jumping up on furniture is often the only honest signal you will get. The rear legs, the hips, the knees, and the lower spine all work hardest when a dog is lifting its body weight upward, which is exactly what a step asks for. Stairs and ottomans are diagnostic events, and refusal is data, not laziness.

Traction is the second half of it. A senior dog on slippery wood or tile loses confidence with every slip, and the fear of slipping can shut down the whole activity even when the joints are only mildly sore. Long nails make it worse by tilting the whole foot and robbing the paws of grip.

And there is an energy piece that is easy to miss: a senior who is not eating well or not digesting well does not build or keep muscle, and muscle is what carries a body up a step. When Mabel started leaving half her food in the bowl and her stools turned soft, she was quietly losing the very strength the stairs demanded. That is why the plan had to address more than just her hips.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first attempt was pure encouragement, I sat on the bed and patted the mattress and called her up like a puppy. She wagged and stayed on the floor. It is the same lesson I had to learn with the stairs: you cannot cheer a hurting animal into movement, and the fact that she wanted to come up but could not should have sent me to the phone that same night.

Then I tried lifting her. All sixty pounds of her, up onto the bed every night for two weeks, until my own back paid the price and she started shrinking away from my hands. Being lifted taught her that bedtime was now something that got done to her, and she grew more reluctant, not less.

My third mistake was a cheap ramp to the bed that flexed under her weight. She stepped onto it once, felt it give, and never trusted it again. A wobbly ramp is worse than no ramp because it converts a physical problem into a trust problem.

What Actually Worked

It started with the vet and an honest described timeline, the second step, the ottoman, the morning stiffness, all of it, without me deciding in advance what it meant. They assessed her joints and her body condition, and we set up a plan that treated the pain and the mechanics separately.

Then I fixed the house. I trimmed her nails so she could actually grip, added a non-slip runner at the base of the stairs and a nightlight, and replaced the flimsy ramp with a sturdy low-platform stair she could step up in flat stages instead of climbing a wall. I also re-measured the feeding: I switched to measured meals twice a day so her weight stayed in a range that her joints could carry.

The piece that tied it together was her condition. Because she had lost her appetite and her stools had gone soft, the vet flagged that she was not absorbing what she ate, and we started her on the Pet Honesty Dog Probiotics Chews for gut and digestive health, the pumpkin ones in the 90-count tub. Within a couple of weeks her appetite came back, her digestion settled, and she started putting on a little quality condition. A senior dog who is digesting her food and holding her muscle is a dog with the strength to climb. The probiotics were not a mobility pill, but they gave her back the fuel, and the stairs came a lot easier because of it.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Measure the thing your pet refuses. If it is one particular step, that is a clue about pain and mechanics, not about age. Then see a vet before you decide it is nothing, because hidden pain is the number one thing owners dismiss in senior animals, and early management is easier than late management.

Fix traction, trim the nails, light the path, and only trust ramps and stairs that do not wobble. And watch the whole animal, appetite, stools, weight, energy. A senior who digests well is a senior who moves. Mabel climbs the ottoman again now, slow but sure, and she sleeps on the bed every night, on her own four legs.
