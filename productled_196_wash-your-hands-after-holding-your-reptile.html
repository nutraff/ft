Do You Actually Wash Your Hands After Holding Your Reptile? Here's the Routine I Follow Now

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

September 20, 2026

The moment that changed my habits was small and ordinary. My nephew, six years old and fearless, held my gecko for a minute, said "he's so cool," and then reached straight for the bowl of crackers on the coffee table. I caught his hand a half-second before he got there, and in that half-second I realized my own routine had been just as sloppy for years, a quick wipe on my jeans, a squirt of hand sanitizer if I remembered, then back to making dinner.

Nobody had ever actually explained to me why it matters. I knew reptiles "carry germs" in the vague way you know to wash your hands after the gym. It took a vet's offhand comment to make me understand that this isn't about being squeamish or phobic, it's about a specific, well-documented risk, and a routine so simple I couldn't believe I'd been skipping it.

Why This Happens

Reptiles can carry Salmonella bacteria in their digestive tract, and it sheds in their droppings, which means it can end up on their skin, their scales, their claws, and every surface their enclosure touches. The bacteria are perfectly normal for the reptile; it doesn't make them sick. The problem is what happens when those germs get from the animal to your hands and then to your mouth, which is exactly what my nephew almost did with the crackers.

Hand sanitizer alone is part of why people think they're covered when they aren't. Alcohol gels kill a lot of germs, but they don't remove the dirt and organic material that can shield bacteria, and good handwashing studies show soap and running water is the more reliable method for this particular risk. A quick sanitizer squirt might actually make things worse by giving you false confidence to touch your face.

And the risk isn't evenly shared. Very young children, older adults, pregnant people, and anyone with a weakened immune system are far more vulnerable to a serious infection, which is why the guidance around reptiles is strict for those groups. For a healthy adult the risk is small and manageable; for a toddler or an immunocompromised family member, "small and manageable" is not good enough. That's the entire reason the routine matters.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My old routine was a wipe on my pants. It does nothing, I now know, it just moves the contamination from my hand to my jeans, where it sits until I touch the fabric again and pick it right back up. "My hands look clean" was never the standard, and denim isn't a sink.

Then I leaned on hand sanitizer, because it was fast and lived on my counter. It's better than nothing, but it's not the answer for reptile handling: without soap and water to physically rinse away the organic matter, you're not getting the surface genuinely clean, and I was using the shortcut as a substitute for the real thing rather than a stopgap when soap wasn't available.

The worst habit was letting my gecko walk on the kitchen counter while I prepped food, "just for a minute," because it was cute and he was contained. That's the single biggest no in reptile-keeping: any surface where food is prepared or eaten is off-limits, always, no matter how clean the animal looks. Cutting that one habit was more important than any product I bought.

What Actually Worked

The routine I landed on is boring, which is exactly why it works. Every single time I finish handling a reptile or cleaning its enclosure, I wash my hands with soap and warm running water for a full twenty seconds, getting between the fingers and under the nails, then dry with a towel that's only used for that. No exceptions for "just a quick touch." The twenty-second count is real; humming a song while you scrub is a decent way to keep yourself honest.

I also made the handwashing station itself easy, because friction is what breaks routines. A pump soap and a dedicated towel right by the sink, with a stack of clean hand towels so I'm never tempted to use the kitchen towel that also touches food. Having it right there, ready, means I actually do it instead of promising to do it later.

For the dirty side of reptile care, scrubbing the enclosure, changing substrate, dealing with droppings. I added disposable gloves and a separate cleaning routine. Gloves for the messy work, gloves off and straight into the trash, then the same soap-and-water wash. I clean the enclosure with reptile-safe products, wipe down the counter and tools afterward, and I keep all of it away from food-prep surfaces so the two worlds never cross. New reptiles get a quarantine period and extra-careful handling, because I can't know what they're carrying on day one.

The family rules fell in line with the same logic. Hands get washed before and after holding, nobody eats while holding a reptile, nobody puts the animal near their face or kisses it, and kids hold reptiles only with an adult right there who washes their hands for them afterward. As for supplies, the soap, the gloves, a reptile-safe cleaner, and a stack of dedicated towels, I keep them all stocked as a set so the whole routine is a single grab instead of a scavenger hunt. That's the other gift a good routine gives you: it removes the excuse to cut a corner when you're busy.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Wash with soap and running water for a full twenty seconds every time you touch a reptile or clean its enclosure, before and after, don't trust a quick wipe or a sanitizer squirt to do a sink's job. Keep reptiles off any food-prep or eating surface, don't kiss them or touch your face while handling, and set stricter rules for kids, older adults, and anyone immunocompromised.

None of this is about fearing your pet. My gecko is fine, I'm fine, and we handle him all the time. It's just that the one thing standing between a healthy hobby and a bad week is the twenty seconds at the sink, and it costs nothing to make those twenty seconds a habit.
