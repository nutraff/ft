My Aussie Turned My House Into a Wrecking Zone — Until I Finally Gave Him a Job

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

January 22, 2026

Walter is a two-year-old Australian shepherd, and for most of his second year he treated my living room like a demolition site. Couch cushion disemboweled. Remote control chewed down to the circuit board. A paperback shredded across the hallway and reorganized into snow. Baseboards gnawed. He did all of it, every time, while I was at work, and every time I came home, he greeted me at the door looking delighted with himself, tail going, as if he'd finished a big project and was proud of the results.

For a long time I thought I had a bad dog. Then it clicked. Australian shepherds are herding dogs, and herding dogs are among the smartest, most driven working breeds on the planet. They were developed to hold together a flock for an entire day, thinking on their feet. When you take that dog and give him an empty house and nothing to herd, he doesn't relax. He invents a job. His job, unfortunately, became redecorating my home one object at a time.

Why This Happens

Herding breeds don't just want exercise, they want purpose. A forty-minute run tires Walter's body for about an hour. What actually tires his brain is work: tracking, solving, deciding. When he doesn't get that, all that mental capacity turns inward on the environment, and the results look like destruction even though the motive is closer to occupational therapy. He wasn't angry and he wasn't anxious, at least not at first. He was under-employed.

There's also a stage factor. Two years is the tail end of adolescence and the start of full adult drive, and a big, athletic dog in that window has more horsepower than a lower-energy breed ever will. On top of that, the destruction itself became a self-reinforcing habit, chewing feels good, and once a dog learns that the couch is a satisfying chew toy, every unsupervised hour is another rep in that habit.

The first thing I had to accept was that I couldn't just remove the behavior. I had to replace it. You can't take away a working dog's job and leave a vacuum; you have to hand him a better one.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first attempt was more physical exercise. I ran Walter twice as far and let him sprint in the field until he was panting. It backfired, as any herding-breed owner will tell you: I was breeding an athlete. The fitter he got, the more exercise he needed to feel satisfied, and the whole thing spiraled upward until no amount of running was enough. Conditioning his body did nothing for the restless brain that was chewing the baseboards.

Next I tried crating him all day while I was at work. That stopped the destruction, mostly, but it also meant he spent his day bored and frustrated in a box, and he came out of it like a spring wound too tight. The behavior didn't go anywhere; it just waited until the door opened.

Then I tried the old standby of scolding him after the fact. I'd come home, see the wreckage, and correct him. Every single time, he just looked confused, because he had no way to connect my late reaction to something he'd done hours earlier. Dog discipline only works within a second or two of the act, so all I was doing was teaching him that my coming home was scary, which is a terrible thing to teach a dog who lives for his owner's return.

What Actually Worked

The turnaround started when I gave Walter real work. I taught him a few herding-style games that we could play in the yard, formal fetch with a specific toy he had to "bring," directional cues left and right, and a simple trick routine that made him think through a sequence. Fifteen minutes of that kind of problem-solving drains him more than an hour of running. I also started feeding him partly through a food puzzle so his brain had a task at every meal, and I built a predictable daily rhythm of exercise in the morning and training in the evening so he could anticipate it instead of waiting all day and boiling over.

But work alone isn't enough if the dog still has unsupervised access to a house full of chewable objects, so I paired the mental stimulation with a serious chew outlet. Walter chews like it's his calling, and every soft toy I'd bought him was filleted within a day. I finally got him the WinTour Tough Dog Toys for Aggressive Chewers, and these are built to survive exactly the kind of determined jaws that had been eating my furniture. That was the switch that mattered most. A powerful chewer will chew, the only question is what. Giving him something that could actually take the abuse redirected the whole habit from the couch to the toy, and he'd work on it for a long stretch while I was on calls. He never got through it, which I still consider a personal victory.

The other half of the fix sounds unserious, and it isn't: I made my house dog-proof in the places that mattered. Walter is smart enough to nose open a bag of treats or a bin of kibble and help himself, and a herding dog who finds a food source will absolutely work out how to get back to it. I moved his food and treats out of reach and into an airtight container, I keep his training rewards in a Glass Terrarium with an Airtight Wood Lid that seals shut and sits up high on a shelf. Naming aside, it's a heavy-lidded storage jar with a genuine seal, and it does two jobs: it keeps treats fresh, and it closes off the reward that would otherwise turn "raid the pantry" into his next favorite job. Out of smell range means out of mind for a dog whose nose runs his schedule.

Between the real work, the tough chew outlet, and the locked-down food, the destruction tapered off over about six weeks and then simply stopped. Walter still needs a job. Now he gets one on purpose.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Stop trying to outrun a herding dog. More physical exercise just builds a fitter, hungrier athlete, and crating him all day doesn't address the restlessness driving the destruction. Give him genuine work, problem-solving games, trick sequences, food puzzles, a real daily training session, and give him a chew that can survive him, because a powerful chewer with no legal outlet will find an illegal one. Dog-proof the food, because a smart working dog will always follow his nose to the next project.

If your dog is destroying things out of genuine separation distress. Howling, escaping, panicking when you leave, that's a different problem entirely, and it's worth talking to a vet or a behaviorist rather than just buying tougher toys. Walter wasn't distressed. He was bored and brilliant. The day I started giving him a job instead of a scolding, he quit demolishing my house and went back to being the best dog I've ever had.
