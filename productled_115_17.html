I'm 17 — My Parents Fight Every Day, and I Just Got a Puppy, Where Should He Actually Live?

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

December 11, 2025

I bought a puppy with my own money three weeks ago, and I did it partly because I wanted something in this house that was mine and happy and did not yell. My parents have been fighting almost every day for as long as I can remember, and the volume has only gotten worse. So now I am seventeen, I have a nine-week-old puppy in a house where two adults argue through walls, and I am trying to figure out the one thing everyone keeps asking me: where should he actually stay?

I have thought about this more than I have thought about anything, because I am the one who has to live with the consequences. If you are in a similar spot, here is what I have figured out so far, honestly, including the part where I had to be real about whether I can do right by a dog in a house like this.

Why This Happens

The first thing I learned is that dogs do not need to understand the words to feel the fight. They read tone, tension, and body language, and a house that is loud and volatile is genuinely stressful for a puppy. A dog that lives in chronic conflict often becomes anxious, jumpy, or clingy, not because it was born that way, but because its home never felt safe. So "where should he stay" is really a question about where he can feel safe and consistent.

The second thing is that puppies need routine and one reliable person, not a rotating cast. In a house where the adults are at war, a puppy can easily get caught in the middle, one parent says no dogs on the couch, the other sneaks him up there, someone feeds him scraps, someone else is too angry to take him out. All that inconsistency is confusing and it slows down training badly. A puppy thrives on predictability, and conflict destroys predictability.

The third piece is harder and I am not going to pretend otherwise: a volatile home is a real factor in whether a dog does well, and it is not something a seventeen-year-old can fully fix. That is not your fault, but it is something you have to plan around rather than wish away.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first instinct was to keep him with me at all times, in my room, on my bed, glued to my side, because I wanted him to feel loved and because honestly I wanted the company. That did not work. He got so attached to being with me that the moment I left for school he would panic, and he never learned to settle on his own. I had accidentally made an anxious dog in three weeks.

Then I tried letting him sleep wherever the mood of the house dictated, sometimes the living room, sometimes with my mom, sometimes with me. That was chaos. He never knew where his spot was, and the shifting made him more nervous, not less. Puppies want a den; they do not want a different den every night depending on who is speaking to whom.

My third mistake was leaning on him too much emotionally. I caught myself treating him as my comfort instead of the other way around, crying into his fur after a bad night, letting him sleep on my chest whenever things got loud. That is not fair to a dog. He cannot be my support animal just because I need one, and it was making him reactive to my moods. I had to separate my need for comfort from his need for a calm owner.

What Actually Worked

What worked was giving him one fixed, quiet home base and enforcing it no matter what was happening in the rest of the house. For us, that is a crate in my room, with a bed, in the corner away from the door, so that when the arguing starts he has somewhere predictable and enclosed to retreat to instead of pacing the house. That crate is his, it is where all his good things happen, and it stays in the same spot every night. Consistency in one small area is more important than a perfect house.

I also took full ownership of his routine, because shared responsibility in a conflicted house is no responsibility at all. I feed him, I walk him, I train him, I clean up after him, on a schedule I keep. Nobody else in the house needs to agree on anything for that to work, because it is my job and I do it the same way every day. The routine is what keeps him feeling secure even when the adults in the room are not.

For the anxious, wound-up moments that came with the noise, I looked for gentle support rather than force. On the loudest evenings, when he was pacing and panting and could not settle, a calming chew helped take the sharpest edge off so he could actually rest instead of vibrating all night. There is a version with melatonin that works well right before bed, when the evening arguments had him over-aroused and unable to switch off. Neither one is a substitute for a calmer home, and I want to be clear about that, they just helped him get through the hard stretches while I worked on everything else.

Finally, I got realistic and made a plan. I talked to a vet about what a puppy actually needs, I lined up a friend who can take him if the house ever becomes genuinely unsafe, and I set rules I do not break: he is never left alone with the chaos, he is never used as a way to get at either parent, and if I ever feel like I cannot give him the consistency he needs, I will find him a home that can. That last one is the hardest thing I have written, but it is what being a responsible owner actually means at seventeen.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Pick one quiet spot in the calmest room you have, make it his fixed den, and own his whole routine yourself so it does not depend on anyone else agreeing. Do not let him sleep in a different place every night, do not use him as your emotional support, and do not become his only comfort or he will fall apart when you go to school. Gentle calming support can help on the worst nights, but the real fix is consistency, and that is on you.

And be honest with yourself. If your home cannot give a dog a stable, safe routine no matter what you do, the kindest option is to find one that can. I love my puppy, and loving him means being willing to put him first even when it is not what I want. So far, I have been able to give him that. If that changes, I will do the right thing, and I hope you would too.
