My Rabbit Was Barely Eating His Hay — Here's How I Got Him Back on His Staple

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

August 24, 2025

I noticed the poops first, because with rabbits that's always where it starts. Biscuit, my Holland Lop, had always left a tidy pile of round, golden, uniform droppings in his litter corner. One morning they were small and dark and irregular, and there weren't as many. He was still bright-eyed and hopping around, still eating his pellets, so I told myself he was fine. That was a mistake, and I got lucky that it was a small one.

What had actually changed was his hay. He'd been eating it lazily for a couple of weeks (picking at it, scattering most of it, and waiting for the pellet bowl instead. When I finally measured it out over a day, he was eating maybe a quarter of what he should. For a rabbit, that is not a small dietary detail. It's the kind of thing that ends in a midnight emergency vet visit, because rabbits that stop eating can go into stasis fast.)

Why This Happens

Hay is not a treat for a rabbit. It is the diet, grass hay should make up roughly three-quarters of everything a rabbit eats, with pellets and fresh greens filling in the rest. There are two big reasons, and both matter. The first is teeth. Rabbit teeth grow continuously for their entire life, and the abrasive silica in hay is what wears them down as the rabbit grinds its way through a pile. Without that constant chewing, teeth overgrow, develop sharp spurs, and cause pain that makes eating hurt, which starts a vicious cycle.

The second reason is the gut. A rabbit's digestive system is built to run on a continuous flow of high-fiber material. Hay keeps things moving and feeds the healthy bacteria in the gut. When fiber intake drops, the gut slows, motility falls, and you're on the road to stasis, a condition where the gut basically shuts down, the rabbit stops eating entirely, and things get dangerous within a day or two. Those small, dark poops I saw were an early warning, not a random blip.

The reason rabbits drift off hay is almost always the same: pellets and treats are tastier and easier. A rabbit will eat a bowl of pellets to the exclusion of hay if you let it, because pellets are calorie-dense and effortless. If I refill the pellet bowl the moment it looks low, I'm training him to wait for the easy food. The hay has to be fresh, it has to be the good quality stuff, and it has to be the thing he can't skip.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first instinct was to buy more hay, so I grabbed the cheapest big bag at the pet store. That backfired. It was dusty and stale, and Biscuit treated it with total contempt, turning his nose up and scattering it. Old, dry hay loses its smell and its appeal, and a rabbit knows the difference instantly even when I can't.

Then I tried bribing him by mixing in more pellets, thinking he'd eat the hay that came with them. That was backwards. I was rewarding him for ignoring hay and giving him exactly what he wanted, which made the problem worse within days. His poops got smaller, not bigger.

I also tried scattering a few treats and carrot chunks through the hay to make it more interesting. He dug out the treats and left the hay untouched. Treats are not a gateway to hay; they're a competing food that wins every time.

What Actually Worked

The turnaround was mostly about making fresh hay the star of the show again, and removing the competition. I switched to a fresh, fragrant timothy hay and stopped refilling the pellet bowl on demand, measured pellets, twice a day, and never a top-up. Biscuit complained for two days and then, with nothing better on offer, started eating his hay in earnest. Within a week his droppings were round and golden again, which is the only progress report a rabbit ever gives you.

To keep him engaged, I turned feeding into a little work. I set out the ChefAide Lick Mat with Suction Cups and smeared it with a thin layer of mashed greens, so he had to lick and work to get his vegetables instead of hoovering them from a bowl. That did two things: it slowed him down, and it made mealtime interesting enough that he stopped pestering me for pellets. Bonus. The mat worked just as well stuck to the side of the fridge for my dog, so it wasn't a single-purpose gadget.

The other piece was growing some of his fresh food myself. I picked up the Unique Gardener Moss Bowl Terrarium Kit and planted a shallow tray of wheatgrass and herbs in it near a sunny window. Within a couple of weeks I was snipping fresh greens straight into his bowl, which meant Biscuit got something genuinely enticing alongside his hay instead of the same tired bag of grocery-store greens. Fresh grass is a magnet for rabbits, and having a steady supply made the whole diet feel less like a restriction and more like a routine he looked forward to.

The combination is what did it: fresh hay as the base, rationed pellets so hay stays the default, and a little fresh green top-dressing that made the bowl worth walking over to. It's been five months and his weight and droppings have stayed steady the whole time.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Learn to read the droppings. They will tell you more about your rabbit's health in five seconds than a whole day of watching him hop around. Small, dark, or oddly shaped poops mean the gut is slowing, and that is your cue to act, fresh hay, no free pellets, and a vet call if things don't turn around fast. Do not wait for a rabbit to stop eating before you take it seriously, because by then you may be in emergency territory.

Buy the freshest hay you can get, and be honest about how much of it he's actually eating rather than assuming a full rack means a full rabbit. Weigh out the pellets instead of free-feeding, because generosity with pellets is what quietly starves a rabbit of fiber. And make the hay the easy, interesting option, a fresh green top-dressing does more than any treat ever will.
