My Rabbit Chewed Through a Charger — a Baseboard, and My Last Nerve, Here's the Foraging Fix

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

August 24, 2026

The evidence was always in the same corner of the living room: a shredded phone charger, a baseboard peeled back to the wood, and a neat pile of cardboard confetti that used to be a shipping box. My rabbit, Biscuit, would be sitting a foot away, grooming a paw, looking innocent. I kept telling myself he was being naughty. He wasn't being naughty. He was bored out of his mind, and chewing was the only job I had left him.

For months I treated it as a discipline problem, a rabbit being "bad", and tried all the things people try with misbehaving pets. None of it worked, because I was aiming at the symptom. The thing that finally stopped the destruction had nothing to do with correcting him and everything to do with giving his mouth and his brain something to do besides remodel my apartment.

Why This Happens

Rabbits are foragers by design. In the wild they spend the bulk of their waking hours nibbling grass, weeds, and woody plants, a nearly constant, low-grade eating-and-searching routine. Their teeth grow continuously and their whole digestive system is built around grazing. When we feed a rabbit a bowl of pellets, he finishes it in three minutes and then sits in a cage or an apartment with hours of nothing to do and a mouth that still wants to work.

That's the trap: a food bowl solves hunger and creates boredom at the same time. A bored rabbit doesn't twiddle his thumbs, he chews. Baseboards, cables, furniture legs, and cardboard are all just stand-ins for the foraging work his body expects. The destruction isn't aggression or a personality flaw; it's an unmet need looking for an outlet, and the more expensive the thing he chews, the more urgently he needed the outlet yesterday.

There's also a social and spatial piece. Rabbits are active at dawn and dusk, they need room to run and leap, and they do best with a predictable routine and a safe territory to patrol. A rabbit kept in a small pen with limited enrichment and irregular interaction will usually channel all that restlessness into chewing. The fix isn't more correction. It's more purpose.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first move was to give him more food. A bigger bowl, topped up constantly, thinking a full rabbit is a calm rabbit. Wrong on every level. He inhaled the pellets, ignored the hay, and got right back to the baseboard with an even bigger appetite for trouble. Pellets digest fast and don't occupy a forager; unlimited pellets just made the boredom worse and the diet poorer.

Then I tried discipline: a loud "no," a squirt bottle, chasing him off the spot. This is the mistake almost every new rabbit owner makes. Rabbits don't interpret punishment the way dogs do. A scary, unpredictable human teaches a prey animal to fear you, not to stop chewing. Biscuit just started doing his damage when I wasn't in the room, which felt like losing even harder.

My third attempt was buying a colorful plastic chew toy assortment, because the store said rabbits love them. He sniffed one, flipped it, and went back to the cardboard. Plastic isn't what a rabbit actually wants to chew, they want fibrous, shreddable, edible-ish material, which is exactly what makes the good stuff (and my baseboards) so appealing.

What Actually Worked

The real shift was rebuilding his environment around foraging instead of feeding. First and most important, I made hay the center of his life: a constant supply of fresh grass hay, refilled daily, because hay is what keeps a rabbit's gut moving and his teeth worn down, and it takes real time to work through. A big hay rack near his litter box turned hours of empty time into hours of grazing, which is the single change that cut the destructive chewing the most.

Second, I stopped delivering his food in one dump. I started scattering his pellets and greens around his space so he had to search for every bite, and I tucked vegetables into cardboard rolls and paper bags so he had to rip them open to eat. That turned mealtime from a three-minute event into a twenty-minute project. A snuffle-style mat did the same job with dry food and gave him something to dig his nose into on days I was busy. When I needed to restock the hay, the foraging tools, and the litter he goes through constantly, I kept one running supply list so we never ran dry mid-week.

Third, I gave him appropriate things to destroy on purpose. Plain cardboard boxes, untreated apple-wood sticks, and a cardboard dig box meant for tearing up replaced every cable and chair leg as a target. The trick was making the "approved" options easier and more rewarding to reach than the forbidden ones, then redirecting him toward them the second he showed interest in the furniture. I also bunny-proofed the room properly, cables in hard plastic conduit, cords lifted off the floor, because no amount of enrichment fixes a setup where the most interesting thing in the room is a live wire.

Finally, I gave him a real routine and real time out. Free run morning and evening during his active hours, a predictable feeding schedule, and a quiet hideaway he could retreat to when he wanted. A rabbit with a territory to patrol and a schedule to trust doesn't need to invent drama. Enrichment, structure, and supervision beat punishment every single time.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Stop treating chewing as bad behavior and start treating it as a signal. Load up on hay, hide his food around the room so he has to work for it, and give him cardboard and untreated wood to wreck on purpose. Then bunny-proof like your cables depend on it, because they do.

Biscuit hasn't touched a charger in months. He still chews constantly, that part never goes away, and it shouldn't, but now he chews the things I set out for him and leaves the walls alone. The day I stopped punishing a forager for foraging and started giving him a life worth foraging in, the destruction just dried up.
