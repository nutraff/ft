My Six-Month-Old Still Won't Tell Me He Needs to Go Out — and the Back Door Makes It Worse

Real solutions for real pet problems · FurTrouble

December 22, 2025

Four months. That is how long I have been taking my puppy out on a rigid schedule, and in all that time he has never once walked to the door and told me he needed to go. Not a whine, not a scratch, not a look, nothing. He will stand in the middle of the living room and just... Go, or wait silently until I happen to take him out. Meanwhile he has also developed a habit of standing at the back door and barking his head off at nothing, which I am now realizing has made the whole problem worse, not better.

I kept thinking he would eventually figure out how to ask. He did not, because asking is not something dogs figure out on their own. It is something you have to teach, on purpose, and the back door was quietly sabotaging the lesson every single day.

Why This Happens

Signaling is a trained behavior, not an instinct. Puppies do not come preloaded with the knowledge that standing by a door means "I need to pee." Some of them babble into it by accident and the owner reinforces it, and those dogs look like naturals. The rest of us take a puppy out on a schedule for months, never wait for a signal, and are then surprised that no signal ever appears. Why would he develop one? I never gave him a reason to. The schedule did all the work, so he never had to communicate.

The back door is a sneaky second problem, and it took me way too long to see it. We let him out the back door to the yard to potty, but we also let him out there to play, to chase squirrels, to bark, and just to be out. So "going out the back door" got split into a dozen different meanings, and none of them was clearly "potty." On top of that, every time he barked at the back door, somebody eventually opened it. Which taught him that barking at that door is a magic button that produces access to the yard. He learned to bark at the door, all right. Just not for the reason I wanted.

The deeper issue is that potty training is really about teaching a dog to hold it, to go in a specific spot, and to ask for access to that spot. If any of those three pieces is missing, you get a dog that just eliminates whenever and wherever, with no way to tell you it needs out. My guy was missing the third piece entirely, and the ambiguous back door was muddling the second one.

What I Tried First — and Why It Didn't Work

My first approach was just to take him out more often and hope a signal would organically appear from the sheer repetition. Four months of that and nothing changed, because repetition without a teachable cue does nothing. He had no idea what I was waiting for, because I was not teaching him anything. I was just standing in the yard a lot.

Then I made it worse by letting him out every single time he barked at the back door, worried he needed to go. That was the mistake that poisoned the well. He quickly learned that barking at the door got him a trip outside, and he started doing it for company, for the squirrels, for boredom, and for a change of scenery. The barking skyrocketed, and his actual potty signals stayed at zero, because why would he bother signaling when barking at the door already worked for everything?

My third attempt was a dog door, thinking he would just let himself out and we would be done. Terrible idea. It removed me from the process entirely, destroying any chance of him learning to communicate with me, and gave him unsupervised outdoor access he mostly used to patrol and bark.

What Actually Worked

The fix was to teach an explicit, unmistakable signal, attached to every single trip outside. I hung a set of potty bells on the door at nose height and taught him to bump them with his nose before every trip out, I would gently guide his nose to them, ring them, and say the word, then immediately open the door. Dogs are brilliant at cause and effect, and it took about two weeks of ringing the bells before every single outing for him to start doing it himself and, more importantly, to understand what it meant.

The second key was to reward the actual potty, not just the trip. I stopped wandering the yard and started taking him straight to one designated spot, waiting him out, and giving a real celebration and a treat the second he went, right there in the yard, not after we came inside. That taught him that going in that spot was the whole point, and that it was a very good thing.

Third, I killed the ambiguity around the back door. The yard stopped being a free-for-all. He goes out on a leash to his spot, does his business, gets rewarded, and comes back in. Playtime happens in a different part of the yard or in the house, separate from the potty run, so "out the back door" finally means one thing. That single change untangled a mess I had accidentally created over months.

And I dealt with the barking at the door itself, because it was drowning out the signal I was trying to teach. Every time he fixated on the back door and started barking for attention or at a squirrel, I used a humane ultrasonic deterrent to interrupt the behavior right as it started, which gave me a half-second window to redirect him to the bells or to a toy. It is not a substitute for training, it is a pattern interrupt, a way to break the barking reflex so the new, correct behavior could actually take hold. Used that way, it sped things up instead of just suppressing the bark.

I kept a strict schedule underneath the training (out first thing, after every meal, after every nap, and before bed, so the new behavior had a safety net while it took hold.)

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Do not wait for a signal to appear, teach one, explicitly, with bells or a cue attached to every single trip out. Make the potty spot separate from playtime so the yard means one thing, celebrate the potty the second it happens, and stop letting your dog out for barking, because that teaches him the door is a magic button for everything except the one thing you want.

My puppy rings the bells now, five months in, mostly when he actually needs to go, and occasionally when he just wants to see if the world is still outside. I will take it. The signal exists. It took four months to build because I spent four months doing it wrong, and I wish I had started teaching it on day one.
